# DPI Deep Dive — Thursday | October 08, 2026

**Focus layer:** L4 — Commerce & Logistics (ONDC, GeM, and the infrastructure that connects sellers to buyers)

**Coverage period:** 1–8 October 2026 (IST)

## Executive summary

This week’s commerce-and-logistics story is less about a new marketplace feature than about the missing work around marketplaces: storage, cataloguing, onboarding, fulfilment, and transport planning. India Post’s ONDC-linked FPO pilot offers an unusually concrete delivery metric; a government factsheet counts artisan beneficiaries onboarded to e-commerce, while Jammu and Kashmir’s trade-promotion body reports district-level registration support. A separate discussion on cooperative commerce and Cabinet approval for an integrated transport authority raise the same question at different scales: can open digital access be matched by accountable, interoperable physical infrastructure?

## Key developments

### 1. India Post turns ONDC discovery into a fulfilment service

A Financial Express report published on 6 October says India Post’s Gujarat pilot has delivered more than 60,000 parcels for five farmer-producer organisations (FPOs) since November 2025. The FPOs hold products at selected head post offices in Ahmedabad, Gandhinagar, Bhuj, and Jamnagar; customers find and order them through MyStore, a buyer application on ONDC. The report describes postal staff handling packaging as well as delivery, with the Department of Posts considering an expansion to about 60 locations. Two FPOs in Tamil Nadu have also listed products, with a Madhya Pradesh launch expected. These are cumulative pilot figures reported this week, not 60,000 deliveries made during the seven-day coverage period.[^1]

That distinction matters. ONDC’s network model can make a seller’s catalogue discoverable across buyer applications, but a discoverable listing does not move a sack of millet cookies or cumin. The postal pilot adds physical storage close to parcel routes, packaging assistance, and last-mile reach—services small producers may not be able to assemble individually. It is a reminder that the “open” part of open commerce is not a substitute for working capital, inventory discipline, packaging quality, returns handling, or customer support.

The report also places the pilot against a larger seller ecosystem: it says more than 7,400 FPOs are on ONDC and over 200 collectives sell through GeM. Those are reported onboarding/listing totals, not evidence that every participant is active or profitable. The public evaluation that would make the 60,000 figure meaningful is still missing: order value and repeat rate, delivery time, cancellations and returns, product damage, seller net receipts after fulfilment costs, and how many of the five FPOs now depend on the service. Scaling to 60 locations should be judged on those outcomes, not only parcel volume.

### 2. PM Vishwakarma’s e-commerce count needs a sales denominator

A Ministry of MSME factsheet published by the Press Information Bureau on 4 October says more than 30,000 PM Vishwakarma beneficiaries have been onboarded on e-commerce platforms, naming GeM, ONDC, Meesho, Amazon, Fabindia, and Sulekha. It also notes an MoU with Amazon to onboard eligible artisans and the One Station One Product (OSOP) initiative, which offers market access to Divyangjan Vishwakarma artisans through dedicated retail outlets at selected high-footfall railway stations. The same factsheet says the scheme had achieved its 30-lakh registration target as it approached its third anniversary.[^2]

This is an important distinction between inclusion inputs and market outcomes. Thirty lakh scheme registrations are not thirty lakh digital sellers; the 30,000 platform figure is not broken down by platform, product, geography, or whether an artisan has completed a sale. The factsheet documents a policy pathway—recognition, skills, tools, credit, digital-transaction incentives, and marketing support—but provides no order conversion, net income, repeat-purchase, or fee data for these newly onboarded sellers. Without that denominator, “onboarded” can mean a first registration rather than a durable route to customers.

The mix of channels is potentially valuable. GeM connects eligible sellers to public buyers; ONDC connects participating seller and buyer applications; commercial marketplaces and railway retail reach different customers. The OSOP outlets also matter because digital discoverability does not solve accessibility, connectivity, or product-readiness barriers for every artisan. A serious evaluation should report how many artisans are active on each channel, what support they receive after registration, whether they control their catalogue and prices, and how much remains with them after commissions, packaging, and shipping.

### 3. J&K takes marketplace handholding to the district level

A 4 October report by JK News says the Jammu and Kashmir Trade Promotion Organisation (JKTPO), under the Union Territory’s Industries and Commerce Department, held export-awareness and e-commerce onboarding drives in Samba, Kathua, Budgam, and Pulwama during September and October. It describes sessions for MSMEs, artisans, weavers, farmers, self-help groups, FPOs, and startups, with assistance to register on ONDC, GeM, and Flipkart. The programme covered export documentation, finance and credit-risk mitigation, procurement, packaging, branding, and digital market access, with resource people from bodies including ECGC, EXIM Bank, FIEO, GeM, and ONDC.[^3]

This is the connective tissue often missing from national platform announcements: a local institution helping a seller turn a product into a compliant listing and understand which channel serves which buyer. But the report describes activities, not measured outcomes. It gives no number of registrations, live catalogues, export-ready products, orders, or businesses that received follow-up support. Nor does it say how an enterprise is referred to logistics, quality certification, or payment-settlement help after the event.

The useful next step is not another count of workshops. JKTPO and the platforms could publish a small, privacy-safe funnel: participants trained; unique businesses assisted; listings approved and still live after 90 days; first and repeat orders; payment time; and median seller proceeds after costs. That would let policymakers distinguish a one-day digital-literacy event from sustained access to procurement and commerce. It would also show whether GeM onboarding produces government tenders and whether ONDC onboarding produces consumer orders—two different forms of market access that should not be collapsed into one “e-commerce” figure.

### 4. Cooperative commerce: a meeting is not a platform launch

Indian Cooperative reported on 7 October that the Ministry of Cooperation’s Secretary, Ashish Bhutani, chaired a meeting with ONDC representatives on 6 October to discuss a proposed e-commerce platform for cooperatives. According to the report, officials and cooperative institutions discussed taking cooperative products to consumers and the practical services needed to make that work: cataloguing, packaging, warehousing, order management, digital payments, onboarding, and last-mile delivery.[^4]

The account signals exploration, not an approved government product. It does not report a decision, budget, implementing agency, timeline, technical architecture, or whether the proposal would be a new marketplace or an assisted route into existing networks. That distinction deserves emphasis because cooperatives already range from large institutions with established brands to small, member-owned producers with little digital capacity. A shared catalogue and logistics-support layer could help the latter; a second closed storefront with its own identity, payment, and fulfilment silos could make them maintain duplicate inventory and software without creating new demand.

The test should be interoperability and cooperative control: can a cooperative publish once and reach multiple buyer applications, retain control over its product and member data, see fees before accepting an order, and access affordable fulfilment? Any new public investment should disclose how it complements ONDC and GeM rather than simply relabeling a portal as “open.” The reported meeting is worth watching precisely because the design decision is still open.

### 5. ITLA proposes a data layer beneath physical logistics

On 6 October, the Union Cabinet approved an Integrated Transport & Logistics Authority (ITLA) as a special-purpose vehicle. The PIB release says the body is intended to coordinate long-horizon transport planning and project appraisal, monitor infrastructure projects above ₹500 crore, and develop a National Transport Data Repository (NTDR). The proposed repository would combine relevant transport and logistics datasets, including GSTN e-way bills, FASTag, Vahan, GPS-based systems, and urban-transport-management data, to support planning and impact assessment.[^5]

This is not a live logistics database or a consumer-facing commerce feature; the release describes a body that will be set up and a repository it will build. Still, it is the week’s clearest cross-layer link. ONDC and GeM can organize discovery and orders, while roads, rail, ports, tolling, and freight data shape whether goods arrive on time and at a viable cost. Better multimodal planning could help explain bottlenecks that seller-level platforms cannot see.

The data-governance questions are equally concrete. The Cabinet note identifies source systems and planning purposes but does not specify the repository’s access tiers, retention rules, aggregation standards, correction process, security controls, or protections for commercially sensitive movement data. “Integration” can mean useful aggregate freight-flow analysis; it can also create a highly linkable view of vehicles, consignments, and business activity. The authority should publish a data-governance framework before operational feeds are connected, with clear limits on reuse and independent audit. Transparency about projects above a threshold is useful, but a repository that affects transport decisions also needs transparent methods and a route to challenge bad or misleading data.

## Trends and implications

Three layers are converging: **market access** (ONDC, GeM, and commercial buyer apps), **seller capability** (catalogues, packaging, onboarding, fulfilment), and **transport coordination** (ITLA’s planned data repository). The week’s most useful evidence comes from activities that touch more than one layer: post offices combining online orders with storage and delivery; artisan programmes combining skills and credit with market channels; and district drives connecting export advice to platform onboarding.

But the evidence is still mostly about inputs—registered sellers, workshops, approved structures, and planned repositories. What residents and producers need is a public outcome ledger: active sellers, successful and repeat orders, fulfilment quality, payment delays, dispute resolution, and net proceeds. GeM should be reported separately from consumer commerce, and listings separately from sales. For logistics data, access and privacy rules should precede broad integration, not follow it.

## Upcoming milestones to watch

- Whether India Post publishes a Gujarat pilot scorecard before expanding beyond its current locations.
- Whether PM Vishwakarma and JKTPO publish platform-level active-selling and seller-income measures, not only onboarding counts.
- Whether the Ministry of Cooperation turns the reported discussion into a documented, interoperable plan with cooperative representation.
- ITLA’s formal constitution, NTDR governance rules, and the methodology for its proposed transport master plan.
