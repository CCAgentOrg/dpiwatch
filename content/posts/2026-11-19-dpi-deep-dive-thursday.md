# DPI Deep Dive — Thursday | November 19, 2026

**Focus Layer:** L4 — Commerce & Logistics: ONDC, GeM
**Coverage Period:** November 12 – November 19, 2026

## Executive summary

The commerce layer's week ran on two clocks: the festive season's transaction counters and the accountability calendar. On the marketplace side, DigiHaat — an ONDC-network platform built around traditional and artisanal products — reported 175.6% year-on-year growth in non-food festive orders, with 76.5% of its October 2026 orders coming from tier-2, tier-3, rural and unclassified markets.[^1] Its delivery backbone, ONDC's Open Delivery Protocol, disclosed network-level scale of 60,000+ merchants and 150,000+ delivery partners, with EV fleet operator Bounce adding vertically integrated electric capacity.[^2] On the finance side, ONDC's financial services chief Hrushikesh Mehta laid out the credit network's next act — buy-now-pay-later, MSME business loans and loans against mutual funds — with 11–12 lenders live and annual lending facilitated crossing ₹400 crore this year, against roughly ₹1 crore in the network's first.[^3] On the logistics side, Delhivery operationalised ONDC-protocol booking of dedicated Indian Railways parcel corridors across 150 manufacturing clusters, cutting Delhi–Bengaluru transit from 78 hours to 42.[^4] And on the governance side, the Central Information Commission ordered GeM — the government's own procurement marketplace, past ₹20 lakh crore in cumulative GMV as of its tenth anniversary in August[^5] — to disclose its internal findings on cartelisation, market concentration and entry barriers, rejecting the platform's blanket commercial-confidentiality objection.[^6] The connective thread: the layer's institutions are deepening (more lenders, more rail, more rural demand) while being asked, in the same week, to open their own books — ONDC to merchants through promised central dispute-and-settlement infrastructure, GeM to citizens through the RTI Act.[^3][^6]

## Key developments

### 1. The CIC orders GeM to open its cartelisation file

On November 18, PTI reported that the Central Information Commission has directed Government e-Marketplace to give an RTI applicant the *general findings* of its internal assessments on cartelisation, market concentration and entry barriers — after GeM acknowledged it analyses repeated allocation of orders to the same sellers and seller groups, but refused to hand over the reports citing commercial sensitivity under Section 8(1)(d) of the RTI Act.[^6] Information Commissioner Khushwant Singh Sethi found the exemption was invoked "in a general and mechanical manner," holding that a bare description of the provision is not sufficient justification, and directed a point-by-point reply with the findings. Along the way, the order pulled back a curtain on what the platform does and does not measure: GeM denied the existence of a "link facility" that would steer procurement or bid links to selected sellers; it said it does not maintain data on how many unique sellers participate per bid or what share of total purchases concentrates with repeat sellers — while stating that 3,36,904 unique sellers have received orders to date. On defence procurement figures (Army, BSF, SSB, CRPF), GeM shifted from Section 8(1)(e) to 8(1)(a) mid-hearing; the CIC demanded a revised, categorical reply there too.[^7]

The analytical significance is an internal contradiction made externally visible. GeM's own tenth-anniversary release advertises AI/ML deployed to "detect cartelisation, collusion, and order splitting" as a governance edge.[^5] The CIC order now asks the platform to disclose what that detection actually found — and GeM's position, in effect, is that the output of its anti-cartel surveillance is commercially confidential from the public whose money is being spent. The RTI reply pegged FY2024-25 procurement at ₹5.42 lakh crore on the platform.[^6] GeM's headline inclusion metric — MSEs fulfilling 45.6% of cumulative GMV — measures who *supplies*; the CIC fight is about measuring who *wins repeatedly*, which is the competition question.[^5] A marketplace cannot celebrate detection capability on launch days and decline disclosure of findings on RTI days. Buyer-side remedies ("view network-related details of bidders") put cartel-detection labour on individual government purchase officers; the commission's order begins the process of making the platform itself accountable for what its own systems see.

### 2. ONDC's credit network picks its next three products

In an interview published November 17, ONDC Chief Business Officer for financial services Hrushikesh Mehta confirmed the credit network's expansion into BNPL/purchase finance at checkout, collateral-free MSME business loans underwritten on GST invoices and order history, and loans against mutual funds via digital lien-marking — with credit cards already live through HDFC Bank as issuer and Bajaj Markets as distributor.[^3] Mehta put the lender bench at 11–12 institutions live with another 10–12 in onboarding, and the growth arc at roughly ₹1 crore of lending facilitated in the network's first year, ₹212 crore in its second, and past ₹400 crore in the current year, targeting ₹1,000–1,200 crore. ONDC's own quarterly newsletter (Pulse, FY26-27 Q2, released this month) grounds the traction: 10,500 loans disbursed in Q2, more than half to new-to-credit or thin-file borrowers, plus 30 asset managers and 126 distributors live on the network's mutual-fund rail — the substrate the loans-against-mutual-funds product will borrow against.[^8]

Two things deserve more attention than the product list. First, the >50% new-to-credit share is the network's real claim to distinctiveness — underwriting on cash-flow and commerce data rather than bureau history is the thing a marketplace consortium can do that an individual lender app cannot — but 10,500 loans a quarter is small change against the rhetoric; the layer remains a pilot at national scale. Second, the product design is quietly regulatory-heavy: BNPL embeds credit into impulse purchase flows at checkout, exactly the pattern RBI's digital lending framework scrutinises; every offer must carry the mandated Key Fact Statement, direct disbursal and cooling-off period regardless of how frictionless the buyer app renders it.[^8] Loans against mutual funds ride SEBI's lien infrastructure, not the RBI's — one product, two regulators, one protocol. An open credit network distributes origination across dozens of buyer apps; the question nobody has yet answered publicly is where consumer redress concentrates when a thin-file borrower is approved by one lender on one app and the harm shows up on another.

### 3. Line-haul moves onto rail, booked through a commerce protocol

On November 18, Delhivery disclosed a strategic partnership to run inter-city express cargo on dedicated Indian Railways parcel corridors, with ONDC's protocol handling automated booking and tracking — the layer's most concrete move yet from hyperlocal last-mile into line-haul.[^4] Per the disclosure and ONDC's logistics release as summarised by market-data desk TradeKaizen: twelve daily contracted corridors (Delhi–Mumbai, Delhi–Bengaluru, Kolkata–Chennai, Ahmedabad–Hyderabad among them) reaching 150 manufacturing and distribution clusters, up from 92 urban centres; Delhi–Bengaluru transit compressed from 78 road hours to 42 rail hours; line-haul freight costs down from ₹0.62 to ₹0.48 per kg-km (about 22%); Q2 FY27 express parcel gross margin at 18.4% with the rail shift projected to add 120–150 basis points; and fleet carbon intensity falling from 128 to 44 grams of CO₂ per tonne-kilometre.[^4][^9]

The structural point is what the protocol is being used *for*. ONDC's logistics layer began as a discovery-and-matching mechanism for last-mile delivery — dozens of local providers, one integration. Here it operates as a booking and interoperability layer over *public* rail infrastructure, which is a different and more interesting proposition: scheduled passenger-priority trains cannot be summoned by an API, so the network's value is orchestration (slot allocation, tracking, handoffs) rather than capacity. That is also where the risks sit — terminal dwell and passenger-priority rescheduling are the known failure modes of rail parcel, and neither the disclosure nor the release quantifies on-time performance yet; the margin and emissions figures are market-desk analysis of the company's own filings, not independent verification.[^4] For the network itself, the watch-item is openness of a different kind: this is currently a bilateral integration benefiting India's largest listed logistics firm. If ONDC publishes corridor-level access so smaller shippers and 3PLs can book the same slots through any participant, the rail move becomes network infrastructure; if it stays bilateral, it is Delhivery's moat wearing a protocol badge.

### 4. Festive numbers show where the network's demand actually lives

DigiHaat's festive-season disclosure on November 17 is the week's most granular look at actual open-network commerce. Year-on-year (October 2025 → October 2026): non-food orders up 175.6%, GMV up 170%, units sold up 129%, customer base up 105.7%, orders per customer up 34.1%.[^1] Category texture matters: Pooja and Festive Needs grew roughly 1,507%, home décor and furnishing 571.5%, regional and traditional foods 74.9% — and month-on-month grocery demand (September → October 2026) rose across salt, sugar and jaggery (~98%), snacks, dry fruits and nuts (91.6%), atta (24.8%) and rice (16.3%). The platform's geographic footprint nearly tripled — 3,833 to 11,089 cities and towns, PIN-code coverage from 4,724 to 8,437 — and tier-2, tier-3, rural and unclassified markets accounted for 76.5% of October's orders. On the supply side, PRERNA Collective, a rural livelihoods collective under Uttar Pradesh's State Rural Livelihoods Mission, reported sales value up 455.4% and orders up 262.5% in the festive month.[^1]

Underneath the platform's own numbers sits network-level infrastructure: ONDC's Open Delivery Protocol — one logistics integration reaching many providers — now counts 60,000+ merchants and 150,000+ delivery partners, with Bounce's September integration adding 12,000+ delivery executives on in-house-manufactured electric fleets, FIFO order matching and sub-15-minute delivery ambitions.[^2] Read together with the Delhivery rail development, the logistics stack is assembling in layers: ODP for the last mile, protocol-booked rail for line-haul. The caveat a consumer analyst must state: these are single-platform growth rates presented amid a festive season, not network-level volumes — ONDC's cumulative transaction counter crossed 500 million only in late July 2026, and its retail vertical has historically been its weakest against mobility and logistics.[^10] What the DigiHaat data *does* establish is where open-network demand concentrates when it works: traditional categories, rural geographies, and government-supported livelihoods supply chains — precisely the "Amazing India" vertical ONDC has now made a strategic priority.[^3]

### 5. ONDC narrows its orbit — and promises the infrastructure it never built

The same Mehta interview carried the week's most consequential strategy disclosure: ONDC has narrowed its focus to four areas — Amazing India (artisan and farmer products), direct-to-consumer brands, travel and transport, and financial services — and is building central infrastructure for dispute resolution and settlement.[^3] Mehta also reframed the network's posture toward the very platforms it was created to counter: large e-commerce and quick-commerce operators are "potential participants" whose thriving ONDC aims to help, not compete with. Set against the network's public trajectory — 500 million cumulative transactions achieved late July 2026, roughly 218 million of them in FY2025-26 alone, with monthly volumes of around 9 million by mid-2026 — the narrowing reads as candour about where open-network economics actually work.[^10][^11]

The dispute-and-settlement announcement deserves to be read as the layer's missing consumer-protection organ, not a back-office chore. Open networks decentralise discovery across buyer apps, seller apps and logistics providers — and in doing so, they diffuse liability: when an order fails across four independent participants, no single platform owns the harm. A centralised dispute and settlement spine is ONDC's implicit admission that protocol-level interoperability without protocol-level accountability is a design gap, three-plus years into retail operations. The governance question follows immediately: ONDC is a Section 8 company owned by its market participants, so the rules for disputes, refunds and settlement will be written by the same entities they bind — the consumer seat at that table has to be constructed deliberately. And the platform-inclusion pivot completes a strategic reversal worth naming: a network launched to dilute platform dominance now courts platforms as participants. The consumer proposition changes shape accordingly — from "an alternative to Big Tech commerce" to "portability across commerce," where the value is exit rights and interoperability rather than price discipline. That is a defensible vision, but it is a different one, and it will be judged by different metrics: not whether Meesho should worry, but whether a seller can move storefronts and a buyer can move histories across the network without penalty.[^3]

## Cross-layer watch

- **L2 — Payments:** ONDC's credit products settle into checkout flows that ride UPI, and their underwriting leans on Account Aggregator consent rails; BNPL-at-checkout on an open protocol will test RBI's digital lending guardrails at the network layer rather than the app layer.[^3]
- **L6 — Governance:** the CIC order is the RTI layer disciplining a DPI marketplace — the same statute-and-commission architecture yesterday's L3 deep dive saw failing at document verification is here working as designed, forcing findings disclosure from GeM's own AI/ML surveillance.[^6]
- **L7 — Trust & Privacy:** ONDC's promised central dispute-and-settlement infrastructure is the trust architecture the network deferred; GeM advertising cartel-detection while declining to disclose findings is the same trust deficit in government procurement clothes.[^3][^6]
- **L5 — Sectoral:** protocol-booked rail freight sits squarely under the National Logistics Pipeline's modal-shift ambitions, and DigiHaat's PRERNA growth shows rural livelihoods missions becoming e-commerce suppliers through open networks.[^4][^1]
- **L1 — Identity:** every one of this week's credit announcements assumes PAN/Aadhaar-based digital KYC as the invisible substrate; thin-file lending is downstream of the identity layer's reach.[^8]
