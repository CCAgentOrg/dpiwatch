# DPI Deep Dive — Thursday | November 12, 2026

**Focus Layer:** L4 — Commerce & Logistics: ONDC, GeM  
**Coverage Period:** November 5 – November 12, 2026

## Executive summary

The commerce layer's week ran from a Delhi chintan shivir to a Delhi corruption chargesheet, and the distance between the two is the story. On November 11, Commerce and Industry Minister Piyush Goyal chaired the Bharat Commerce Chintan Shivir on trade digitisation, where ONDC's DigiDukaan kirana-procurement rail reported over 21,000 connected retailers and 450-plus brands across three cities — the B2B rail now carrying the network's growth narrative as the consumer-marketplace thesis strains.[^1][^2] The DPIIT data trail explains the pivot: cumulative transactions crossed 500 million in July 2026, but the base was just 0.2 million in FY23, and the fastest-growing volume now comes from metro tickets and ride-hailing rather than shopping carts.[^4] Chennai's October ticketing ledger made that concrete: the ONDC channel sold 13.95 lakh metro journeys in a single month, the largest app-based digital channel in the system.[^6] GeM's week was about inclusion mechanics — SC/ST supplier filters before 30 summoned CPSEs, vigilance workshops in Gandhinagar, a Stand-Up India credit extension — and then, on November 12, the Anti-Corruption Branch's ₹842-crore Delhi health-procurement chargesheet demonstrated exactly how digitised procurement gets gamed when tender design stays human: 448 X-ray machines were planned; a tender for two was uploaded on GeM.[^13]

## Key developments

### 1. The Shivir and the pivot: DigiDukaan carries the network's story now

The week's anchor event was the Bharat Commerce Chintan Shivir on trade digitisation, chaired by Piyush Goyal on November 11, where ONDC disclosed that DigiDukaan — the network's kirana-as-buyer procurement rail — had connected over 21,000 neighbourhood retailers with more than 450 brands across Hyderabad, Jaipur and Mumbai as of September 2026, up from roughly 12,000 in the previous quarter.[^1][^2] The trajectory is steeper than the headline: DPIIT's written reply in the Rajya Sabha in August recorded DigiDukaan at 13,000-plus stores across just two cities; adding Mumbai and one quarter took the count past 21,000.[^4] ONDC's own channels spent the week retailing the proof points — Hyderabad leads with 16,000-odd retailers, monthly repeat-order rates of 73–78%, and an in-house survey finding 40% of retailers reporting lower inventory holdings for DigiDukaan-procured stock — with a retailer testimonial video pushed on November 11 itself.[^2][^3]

The context that makes this a pivot story rather than a victory lap: DPIIT's August numbers show the network scaled from 0.2 million transactions in FY23 to 218 million in FY26 before crossing 500 million cumulative in July, with more than 2 lakh retail merchants and over 10 lakh service providers in mobility and logistics.[^4] Commentary on X was blunt about what that curve means — the core e-commerce marketplace thesis did not take off, so the network has leaned into ride-hailing, transit ticketing and B2B procurement to build volume.[^5] The Shivir's five deliberation areas — a common digital identity for kirana retailers, shared logistics and micro-warehousing, formal credit against digital procurement records, plug-and-play distributor-management connectors, and realigned commercial incentives — read as an admission that the hard part of digitising 1.4 crore kiranas is not the ordering app. It is the money, the trucks and the margins of everyone in the existing distribution chain. Until incentive realignment is published as a commitment rather than a discussion area, 21,000 of 1.4 crore stores is a promising rail, not a transformed trade.

### 2. Metro tickets are the volume engine — Chennai's October ledger proves it

The most concrete transaction data published this week came not from ONDC but from a metro operator using it. Chennai Metro recorded its highest-ever monthly ridership in October — 1.08 crore passenger journeys — and its channel breakdown shows the ONDC platform accounted for 13.95 lakh of those journeys.[^6] That makes the network channel the system's largest app-based ticketing channel, roughly matching the next four app channels combined: WhatsApp ticketing at 4.74 lakh, the CUMTA-backed Chennai One app at 4.26 lakh, Paytm at 3.35 lakh and PhonePe at 1.76 lakh.[^6] Only paper QR tickets sold at station counters (22.52 lakh) moved more journeys.

Scale that against DPIIT's August disclosure — nearly 80% of India's metro ticketing inventory is available on the network, and as of June 2026 ONDC facilitated more than 3.7 lakh public transport trips daily across 35-plus buyer applications spanning nine metro systems and four city bus entities — and a structural picture emerges: transit is now the network's highest-frequency surface.[^4] The analytical caveat matters. A ₹20–40 metro ticket is not a shopping cart, and blending transit tickets with retail orders flatters the network's headline transaction counts while telling consumers little about commerce GMV. What the transit numbers genuinely prove is habit formation and interoperability utility — a commuter discovering network-based ticketing through one buyer app is acquiring an open-commerce behaviour that retail can later harvest. The logistics side deepened this week too: TCI Express, a listed express-distribution company with Q2 FY27 EBITDA of ₹43 crore, disclosed it has gone live on ONDC and Unicommerce, with ONDC-related expansion named in its forward plans — a mainstream logistics provider treating the network as distribution infrastructure rather than pilot theatre.[^7] One design note for the consumer ledger: CMRL's 20% fare discount covers NCMC cards and specific digital QR channels (WhatsApp, Paytm, PhonePe, the CMRL app's stored-value pass) per the published breakdown — incentives are being assigned channel-by-channel, not network-wide, and how the ONDC channel is treated in fare policy is worth tracking.[^6]

### 3. The post office becomes the dark store: FPO e-commerce scales past Gujarat

The agriculture ministry and India Post are expanding the model that turns designated post offices into dark stores for farmer producer organisations, after the Gujarat pilot expanded beyond its Ahmedabad origin and rolled out in Tamil Nadu and Madhya Pradesh.[^8] FPOs stock produce at post offices, list it on the MyStore app — an ONDC marketplace member — and India Post handles packaging and dispatch at weight-based bulk-discount rates. The Gujarat pilot now runs across seven locations with five FPOs; Tamil Nadu has onboarded two (Maamadurai FPC and Varagananthi FPC) and Madhya Pradesh one (Jay Kisan Unnat PC), with Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh and Maharashtra next and roughly 10,000 FPOs in the addressable set.[^8] The wider scaffolding: over 7,400 FPOs have joined the ONDC ecosystem and more than 200 farmer organisations sell through GeM, while agricultural produce from collectives is reaching consumers via major platforms including Amazon and Flipkart.[^9] DPIIT's August numbers add the physical layer — under the Amazing India initiative, more than 800 independent sellers and eight aggregator entities representing over 1,500 FPOs are onboarded, and seven warehouses have been operationalised in partnership with India Post and DGCPack.[^4]

The DPI significance is structural: 164,999 post offices, over 90% of them rural, are being repurposed as fulfilment infrastructure for open-network commerce — public logistics capacity lowering the minimum viable scale for direct-to-consumer farm retail. The inclusion thread extended to artisans this week: the eSARAS platform's 1,400-plus products by women self-help-group artisans were announced for wider market access, with ONDC and UMANG integration named as the expansion route.[^14] The failure mode to watch is inventory physics. Post offices were built for letters, not shelf-life, and the pilot's product lists — pulses, groundnut, pickles, chilli powder — are conspicuously shelf-stable. Cold-chain produce remains absent, and until it isn't, this is a dry-goods channel with a freshness problem deferred.

### 4. GeM's inclusion week: filters, targets and a credit lifeline

GeM's week ran on two tracks — expanding who sells, and policing how buying happens. On the first: the MSME Ministry convened a review at the Dr. Ambedkar International Centre on November 5, chaired by Joint Secretary Mercy Epao, bringing 30 public sector undertakings — BHEL, Bharat Electronics, NTPC, Airports Authority of India, BEML and Coal India among them — together with the Department of Public Enterprises, NSIC and GeM to accelerate compliance with the mandated 4% procurement target for SC/ST micro and small enterprises.[^10] Epao reported procurement from SC/ST MSEs up more than 36 times in FY26 since the scheme's inception — a figure that is impressive and, off a small base, deliberately hard to read — and unveiled an Entrepreneur Awareness and Special Vendor Development Program. GeM's Additional CEO Ajit Chavan outlined the platform mechanics: dedicated marketplace filters to identify SC/ST MSE sellers, and a Nodal Officer for CPSE handholding.[^10] The credit complement arrived on November 11, when the Cabinet extended Stand-Up India to 2031 with a ₹4,111-crore corpus to support 2.5 lakh more entrepreneurs.[^11]

On the second track: Gujarat's state-level vigilance workshop on preventive vigilance in government procurement, reported November 6 during Vigilance Awareness Week, drilled procurement officers on e-tendering thresholds, effective GeM use, verification of Make-in-India content claims, and specifically warned against splitting procurement quantities to bypass procedures and clubbing unrelated items into single tenders.[^12] Read together, the two tracks expose the layer's real architecture: marketplace plumbing (filters, nodal officers, credit) is the part DPI can build, but tender design — the eligibility criteria and lot-sizing where exclusion and collusion actually live — remains a human craft practised in workshop rooms. A discovery filter helps a buyer find an SC/ST seller; it does nothing if the tender was drafted so no competitor could qualify.

### 5. The counterfactual ledger: how digitised procurement gets gamed

The week's counterpoint arrived on November 12, when Delhi's Anti-Corruption Branch chargesheet in the alleged medical procurement scam detailed an estimated ₹842-crore loss to the government.[^13] The named accused include the Central Procurement Agency's Head of Office, the DGHS chief and a former Deputy Controller of Accounts, alongside two firms allegedly owned by pharma trader Rajiv Rangila. The mechanics matter for the commerce layer. The same X-ray machine that manufacturer Prognosis supplied to other government entities at ₹14.9 lakh inclusive of taxes was billed to the CPA at ₹37.8 lakh by F-Med — the only company to qualify. Although the CPA planned to procure 448 machines, a tender for just two was uploaded on the GeM portal, allegedly to restrict competition. The alleged kickbacks to the CPA's Head of Office total ₹300 crore. The tell is in the payment ledger: bills from the favoured firms were cleared within a day or two while other suppliers reportedly waited nearly two years; hospital linen worth ₹25 crore was billed at ₹75 crore, ORS sachets valued at ₹1.25 crore at ₹7.5 crore, surgical items worth ₹20–25 crore at nearly ₹100 crore.[^13]

The lesson is not that GeM failed — it is that GeM was never designed to be the control point where this scheme gets caught. The platform digitises catalogue discovery, ordering and payment; tender specification, eligibility criteria and approval remain the buyer's craft, and the chargesheet alleges every one of those levers was pulled by hand — turnover and experience criteria set "exceptionally high", tender committee members allegedly threatened into signing, bills processed at a velocity no legitimate supplier experiences. A marketplace is not a protocol against collusion. If GeM's AI-driven catalogue validation, bid health scores and anomaly detection — the governance features showcased at GeM Samvad last month — are to mean anything, payment-velocity asymmetry (one supplier paid in 48 hours, others in 700 days) is exactly the anomaly class they should be flagging to auditors, not just to dashboards. For the consumer, this is the layer's cost question made flesh: every rigged rupee of health procurement is a tax paid twice — once at purchase, once at the clinic.

## Cross-layer connections

Three threads tie the commerce week to the rest of the stack. L4 to L2 (payments and credit): the week's inclusion push — SC/ST filters, Stand-Up India's extension, GeM SAHAY and TReDS invoice financing, DigiDukaan's procurement-linked credit on the Shivir agenda — is one bet repeated four times, that transaction records can underwrite small businesses; those records ride UPI and bank rails, and a kirana's order history is now a credit file. L4 to L7 (privacy and trust): the DPDP Rules' consent-manager provisions take effect on November 13 — the day after this window closes — and commerce records generated on open networks will increasingly sit inside that consent architecture, with the eMudhra certificate probe still running underneath the eSign layer that validates commerce contracts. L4 to L5 (sectoral infrastructure): a single metro system now runs two interoperability stacks side by side — CUMTA's Chennai One unified ticketing at 4.26 lakh journeys and the ONDC channel at 13.95 lakh — and which stack absorbs the other's commuter relationship is a live question for every city building unified mobility apps.[^6]

**What to watch next week:** whether DigiDukaan publishes FMCG commitments on incentive realignment and its next quarterly retailer count; GeM's order-flow response to last month's fee cuts; the Delhi ACB case's judicial progression and any GeM control changes on tender validation and payment-velocity monitoring; India Post's FPO rollout into Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh and Maharashtra; and how the Delhi health procurement scandal reshapes the vigilance curriculum Gujarat was teaching the same week.
