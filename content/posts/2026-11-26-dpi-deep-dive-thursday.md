# DPI Deep Dive — Thursday | November 26, 2026

**Focus Layer:** L5 — Sectoral Infrastructure: ABHA/ABDM, AgriStack, eCourts
**Coverage Period:** November 19 – November 26, 2026

## Executive summary

The sectoral layer's week ran on three registers: registries widening, evidence arriving, and politics pushing back. Pharmacists joined India's digital health registry as the Pharmacy Council of India's DIGI-PHARMED portal went live on November 24, completing the Healthcare Professionals Registry's march through the medical profession.[^1] Cancer screening got a longitudinal rail when the National Cancer Grid's VISHWAS initiative launched with an explicit brief to link screening records to ABHA — the health ID stack now hovering just under 99 crore accounts.[^2][^3] The week's most honest document was a study: IIHMR's multi-state time-motion measurement of ABDM's Scan and Share service, published November 18 at the window's edge, put real numbers — and real caveats — on the mission's most-cited convenience feature.[^4] On the farm side, AgriStack reached the floor of the Odisha Assembly on November 25, where the minister had to clarify that the Farmer ID is *not* mandatory to sell paddy — a striking contrast with Chhattisgarh, whose fully-mandatory digital procurement season is now live.[^5][^6] And the justice layer built and warned in the same week: the Mediation Council of India held its first meeting on November 23 with digital systems on its agenda,[^7] while the Chief Justice of India branded digital arrest scams "digital dacoity" on the eve of Constitution Day.[^8]

## Key developments

### 1. Pharmacists enter the registry: DIGI-PHARMED goes live

On November 24, the Pharmacy Council of India issued its go-live circular for the NHA's HPR DIGI-PHARMED portal, putting the country's pharmacists onto the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission's Healthcare Professionals Registry, with a user manual walking registrants through the onboarding.[^1] The move completes a long registry build-out: doctors came first, then nurses and allied workers, and pharmacists — the professionals who actually dispense the medicines in every prescription — were the last major cadre standing outside the HPR.

The consumer stakes are easy to state and easy to miss. A verified pharmacist identity is the precondition for ABDM's pharmacy-side workflows: prescription records that flow to a medicine, dispense events that attach to a patient's health record, and a national directory that lets a patient in a strange city confirm the person behind the counter is who the system says they are. Registration is voluntary, and the Circular's own framing — a user manual first, incentives later — suggests NHA knows pharmacy adoption will be slower than hospital-side ABHA creation, which the state has been able to push through OPD queues.

The sharper question is what the registry will be *used* for. HPR entries make professionals machine-addressable; the same infrastructure that lets a hospital verify a pharmacist can let an insurance claim engine audit dispensing patterns. Nothing in the circular says anything about that second use — and as with the doctor registry before it, the consent architecture governing who queries the registry, and for what, remains defined by the operators rather than by published, contestable rules.

### 2. Screening meets the record: NCG VISHWAS and the ~99-crore rail

On November 21, Union Health Secretary Punya Salila Srivastava launched NCG VISHWAS — Videos to Support Health, Wellness, Awareness and Self-care — at the National Cancer Grid's 2026 annual meeting in Mumbai: an AI-enabled patient education initiative covering breast, cervical and oral cancers in Hindi, Marathi, Tamil and English, to be disseminated through the Grid's hospital network.[^2] The initiative itself is content. The mandate around it is infrastructure: the Secretary explicitly stressed linking screening records with ABHA for continuity of care, noting that individuals above 30 are being screened for common non-communicable diseases at 1.87 lakh Ayushman Arogya Mandirs, and that some 153 crore cancer screenings have been done under national programmes.[^2][^9]

The scale figures she quoted are the health layer's new normal: around 99 crore ABHA IDs created and about 100 crore health records linked.[^2] The ABDM dashboard corroborates the trajectory — 98.99 crore ABHA accounts as of November 19, with 45.26 lakh created that month alone, keeping the 100-crore round number in sight.[^3] Nadda, addressing JIPMER's convocation the same day, urged young doctors to adopt electronic prescriptions and help digitise records under ABHA — the message to the profession matching the message to the system.[^10]

The analysis point is that cancer is the best test case for consent-based record linking. Screening is a decade-long relationship: a positive oral screening at an Arogya Mandir needs to surface at a district hospital's biopsy, an oncology centre's treatment plan, and a PM-JAY empanelled hospital's claim — across years and states. That is exactly the longitudinal-record promise ABHA was built for, and exactly the flow where a missing or mis-matched record becomes a missed diagnosis. With ~99 crore IDs and ~100 crore records, the binding constraint has quietly moved from creation to *linkage quality* — how many of those 100 crore records are clinically legible to the next facility, and how many are orphaned PDFs. VISHWAS's outcomes will be a good place to watch that ratio.

### 3. The evidence arrives: Scan and Share, measured

The week's most useful health-layer document didn't come from NHA at all. On November 18 — the window's edge, and days before NHA's own national review — IIHMR Bangalore and IIHMR Delhi published in *BMC Health Services Research* a multi-state time-motion study of ABDM's Scan and Share service, the QR-code flow that lets patients share demographics from their ABHA at OPD registration instead of filling forms.[^4]

The design is the first of its kind at this scale: eight public secondary and tertiary facilities across Maharashtra, Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan and Haryana, observed in January 2026 during peak OPD hours, with 1,565 patients timed through entry-to-counter (T1) and counter-to-token (T2) intervals — 631 Scan and Share users, 317 Facilitated users, 617 manual registrants.[^4] The results are real but modest at the median: Scan and Share users waited a median 1.0 minute at the queue (mean 1.2 minutes) against 2.0 minutes median for manual users — whose *mean* was 7.2 minutes, dragged up by bad sites. The statistical significance is strong (p < 0.001, rank-biserial r = 0.41); the clinical magnitude at T2 — time at the counter itself — was negligible, with identical 1.0-minute medians across all three groups. The biggest absolute win was Uttar Pradesh, where manual-registration mean waits of ~23.5 minutes collapsed to ~1.5 minutes under Scan and Share.[^4]

The authors are admirably blunt about the limits: registration pathways were self-selected, so this is an observed association, not a causal estimate; site variation was large; and whether facility congestion explains the differences needs studies that actually measure queue length.[^4] Their policy conclusion — context-sensitive scale-up, prioritising high-volume facilities — is the right one, and it doubles as the layer's homework. NHA's own count of ~24 crore Scan and Share tokens against ~99 crore ABHA IDs implies fewer than a quarter of health IDs have ever generated a token: the identity exists, the usage is thin. For a mission whose leadership keeps saying the next phase is "adoption and effective utilization,"[^2] independent measurement is precisely what the adoption phase needs — the same argument this desk made about UPI's decline-rate data in the payments layer. The ask is now identical: publish the operational series, not just the cumulative milestone.

### 4. AgriStack at the mandi gate: Odisha's floor fight against Chhattisgarh's mandate

AgriStack's fortnight of kharif procurement produced its sharpest political test this week. In the Odisha Assembly on November 25, during an adjournment motion, Opposition Deputy Leader Prasanna Acharya attacked the government over AgriStack registration hurdles and mandis that hadn't opened: in Sambalpur, harvested paddy had already reached markets while not a single procurement centre had opened; registration was, he said, complex enough to keep genuine farmers out; farmers were protesting in Bargarh and Sambalpur; and 28 lakh metric tonnes of rice sat unsold because FCI hadn't lifted it — with mills warning they may stop accepting paddy altogether.[^5]

Supplies Minister Krushna Chandra Patra's reply is the quotable state-level position: AgriStack registration has *not* been made mandatory for selling paddy this kharif; procurement follows last year's rules; 22.27 lakh farmers have registered; the state paid ₹3,100 per quintal and disbursed ₹30,362 crore across 97.94 lakh metric tonnes last year; mandi-opening dates are set district-by-district by the DLPC.[^5] A ruling-party legislator spent November 26 calling the opposition's remarks misleading on the same point.[^11] The Times of India's headline captured the assurance: "AgriStack ID not mandatory to sell paddy."[^6]

Now hold Chhattisgarh beside it. There, for the Kharif Marketing Year 2026–27 that began November 15, an AgriStack Farmer ID linked to the land parcel's khasra-level Farm ID is *compulsory* to sell at MSP at all — eligibility determined strictly by digital crop surveys, biometric authentication at the centre, AI-based image validation against fraudulent entries, 100 per cent QR-code scanning tracking every rice bag from mill to FCI to fair-price shop, payments by direct digital transfer, and tokens booked from home on the Tunhar Token app (available since November 10).[^12][^13] Over 27.5 lakh farmers are registered across 3,100 centres buying at ₹3,100 per quintal up to 21 quintals per acre; the loan book (Rin Pustika) is gone.[^12][^13] India's two big paddy states are running a controlled experiment in adoption strategy — persuasion with a non-mandatory ID in Odisha, compulsion with a digital chokepoint in Chhattisgarh.

The national backdrop sharpens both bets. Farmer IDs crossed 11 crore by late October, PMFBY crop insurance has been Farmer ID-mandatory for land-owning farmers since an August 2026 decision, and the DPDP clock is running: registered consent managers have been permitted since November 13, 2026, with full obligations biting May 13, 2027 — meaning AgriStack's data-sharing architecture must soon live inside a statutory consent regime.[^14] The consumer-protection question both states defer: when a digital crop survey misrecords acreage, or a name mismatch with a land record blocks a registration, the farmer's remedy is a helpdesk and a camp — Odisha's assembly debate is what it looks like when that remedy layer lags the identity layer. Watch the district-level grievance counts from both procurement seasons; they will tell you which adoption strategy produces less exclusion per quintal.

### 5. Justice infrastructure: the Mediation Council convenes, the CJI warns

The justice layer moved on both fronts this week. On November 23, the Department of Legal Affairs convened the first meeting of the Mediation Council of India at Kartavya Bhavan-2, chaired by Dr. Rajiv Mani — MCI Chairman and Law Secretary — with the ministry's stated agenda including training and capacity building, *promoting online mediation and developing digital systems* to make dispute resolution more accessible.[^7][^15] The first meeting matters because the Mediation Act, 2023 expressly permits online mediation with the parties' written consent subject to confidentiality safeguards — yet the Government told Parliament in February 2026 that the provision had not been notified.[^16] A Council that operationalises the digital side would close the gap between the statute and the practice; until notification, the online-mediation framework remains built but not switched on. The Council convenes against a heavy backdrop: eCourts Phase III's ₹7,210 crore paperless-courts programme runs to 2027, over 753 crore pages of court records are digitised and 31 virtual courts function, while NJDG figures laid before Parliament in August counted nearly 4.99 crore pending cases in subordinate courts alone.[^16][^17]

The week's counterweight came from the top of the same institution. Speaking in a Doordarshan interview on the eve of Constitution Day, Chief Justice of India Surya Kant branded digital arrest scams — fraudsters impersonating law enforcement to extort citizens over video calls — as "digital dacoity," urged courts to adapt swiftly to AI-enabled crime and cross-border disputes, and disclosed that benches led by him are running suo motu monitoring of digital arrest rackets, directing messaging platforms and cyber authorities to tighten safeguards and expedite victim refunds.[^8] His framing that judicial innovation must stay anchored to the Constitution's text and the basic structure doctrine is the layer's own governance model in miniature: technology adoption inside explicitly named limits.

Read together, the two events describe a layer that has learned its lesson faster than most. Justice digitisation has proceeded in Phase III with the risks named alongside the tools — live transcription, AI translation, virtual courts — while the judiciary itself polices the crime wave the digital rails produce. The unresolved item is the same one flagged in the L7 coverage: the DPDP Act's first constitutional hearing arrives December 9, and every sectoral stack built on consent — ABHA's record-sharing, AgriStack's data-sharing, DigiLocker's verification flows — inherits whatever framework the court upholds.[^18]

## Cross-layer connections

The week tied L5 into four other layers. Upward, ABHA's screening-linkage mandate leans on L1: every ABHA minted through Aadhaar biometrics inherits the identity layer's accuracy and its disputes. Sideways, AgriStack's procurement rails touch L2 — Chhattisgarh's direct-to-bank-account payments are DBT at MSP scale — and L3, since farmer documents ride the same issuer-registry logic as DigiLocker. Downward, the L6 grievance layer is already straining: farmer and rural-worker organisations backing the Samyukta Kisan Morcha's November 26 protest claim lakhs of MGNREGA grievances on CPGRAMS were "falsely closed without any action" — an activist claim, not a finding, but the sectoral stacks are exactly where the grievance volume will land.[^19] And L7 gates everything: consent managers since November 13, DPDP full obligations May 13, 2027, the Supreme Court on December 9.[^14][^18]

**Watch items:** how quickly pharmacist HPR registrations climb after the November 24 go-live; Odisha's mandi-opening lag in Sambalpur and Bargarh through December; the first consent-manager registrations under DPDP and their sectoral clients; and the December 9 hearing, which redraws the consent architecture under every registry named here.
