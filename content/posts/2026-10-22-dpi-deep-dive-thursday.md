# DPI Deep Dive — Thursday | October 22, 2026

**Focus Layer:** L4 — Commerce & Logistics

**Coverage Period:** October 16–22, 2026 (seven calendar days)

## Executive summary

This week’s most consequential commerce developments sit around the marketplace, not inside a newly announced ONDC or GeM feature. India and New Zealand agreed to exchange trade-origin data electronically; MoSPI refreshed a public infrastructure dashboard; inland waterways gained a clearer digital navigation layer; and the government described new rail-and-road onion distribution and artisan market-access efforts. Together they show the real shape of a commerce stack: discovery and procurement need documentary trust, physical movement, usable information, and routes to customers. The caveat is equally important: official announcements describe systems and intended benefits, but offer little evidence here on trader adoption, delivery performance, or income gains.

## Key developments

### 1. India–New Zealand: trade-origin data moves toward electronic exchange

CBIC and the New Zealand Customs Service signed a Memorandum of Cooperation on electronic exchange of origin data for goods. The agreement was signed on October 19; the India–New Zealand free-trade agreement entered into force on October 20; and the government announced the MoC on October 21. The stated purpose is advance electronic exchange of origin information to support customs cooperation and implementation of the FTA.[^1]

This is a useful example of commerce infrastructure extending beyond a buyer-seller network. Preferential tariffs depend on proving that goods meet rules of origin. If customs administrations can exchange the relevant data in advance, traders may face fewer information gaps and greater predictability when claiming FTA treatment. In practice, that could connect document exchange to logistics: a shipment’s border clearance is part of its delivery journey, not an administrative afterthought.

But the public announcement is a commitment, not a technical service description. It does not specify the data fields, exchange protocol, access controls, error-correction process, launch date, or performance measures. Nor does it show that traders can already see faster clearance. The next meaningful evidence would be an implementation note and measured clearance outcomes—not another statement of intent. This is a cross-border trade rail, not an ONDC or GeM expansion.

### 2. PAIMANA adds a public measurement layer for infrastructure

MoSPI’s October 16 update expanded the PAIMANA Performance Monitoring Dashboard to 184 indicators, adding 21 and refreshing 41 across power, aviation, ports and waterways, and roads. Its stated framework groups indicators around access, quality, fiscal cost and revenue, utilization, and affordability. The release reported major-port cargo at 390.4 million tonnes, up 7.2% year-on-year for April–August 2026, and air cargo at 18.6 lakh tonnes, up 12% over the same period. It also reported 2,236.3 km of National Highway projects awarded in April–September, 62.9% above the comparable period.[^2]

A public dashboard can make the logistics system easier to question: are ports being used, is capacity reaching regions, and what is the fiscal cost? But the measures must not be blurred. Cargo handled is not proof that freight reaches a small seller cheaply; highway kilometres awarded are not kilometres completed; and a dashboard’s official series is not an independent audit. The dashboard’s value depends on transparent definitions, revision histories, and granular enough data to compare time, region, and service quality.

One cross-layer detail matters: the same release reports 7,52,113 km of optical fibre laid by August, with 29,256 km added in the first five months of the fiscal year. Connectivity is a prerequisite for digital market access, but coverage alone cannot tell us whether a seller has affordable, reliable service or can operate a catalogue, accept orders, and resolve disputes. PAIMANA is useful as a measurement surface; it is not a substitute for those outcome measures.

### 3. Inland waterways: navigation information becomes part of the freight network

A PIB backgrounder published October 16 describes electronic navigation charts prepared for 18 operational and tidal waterways and made available through PANI and the IWAI Vessel Tracker. It also places those tools alongside hydrographic surveys, fairway maintenance, weather and depth information, and riverine lighthouses intended to support navigation around the clock. The government reports 111 National Waterways spanning 20,187 km, of which 32 are operational; inland-waterway freight reached 218 million tonnes in 2025–26, compared with 29 million tonnes in 2014–15.[^3]

Digital navigation data is not decorative. On a river, changing depth and weather can determine whether a vessel can move safely, when it can move, and whether a cargo schedule is credible. Charts and vessel information can reduce uncertainty for operators and make water transport more usable as part of a wider logistics chain. That matters for commerce because a lower-cost mode is only a real option if traders can plan around it.

The limits should stay visible. Eighteen charted waterways are not the whole national network, and 32 operational waterways do not mean consistent service on every route. Freight growth is a government-reported aggregate, not a measure of punctuality, price, safety incidents, or the share of small businesses using the mode. The next test is whether route-level information is current, accessible to operators, and linked to measurable reliability—not merely whether a portal exists.

### 4. Kanda Express: a price-stabilization chain still depends on the last mile

On October 16, the Department of Consumer Affairs announced the first Kanda Express movement to Agartala: about 440 tonnes of buffer onion, expected to arrive by October 18. The release said two rail rakes carrying 840 and 452 tonnes, plus 44 truck consignments carrying 1,513.46 tonnes, had supplied locations across Assam, Tripura, and Nagaland. The planned reach included seven of Tripura’s eight districts.[^4]

This is not a digital-commerce launch, and it should not be sold as one. It is a useful stress test for the physical side of public market infrastructure: a centrally managed buffer stock must move by rail, transfer to road, reach local markets, and ultimately affect availability and prices. The government’s announcement gives a route and tonnage snapshot, but it does not establish that the expected Agartala consignment arrived on time, how much reached retail outlets, or whether local prices changed as intended.

The accountability opportunity is straightforward. Publish dispatch, arrival, district-level release, and market-price data together, with dates and consistent units. That would let citizens distinguish a train’s arrival from actual last-mile supply. It would also connect logistics reporting to the consumer outcome the intervention is meant to protect: affordable access, not simply tonnes moved.

### 5. Shilp Samagam puts artisan sales beside the promise of e-commerce

A Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment release on October 20 announced an eight-day Shilp Samagam Mela in Guwahati, running October 21–28. It said 75 artisans, including 31 women, would display about 40 varieties of handloom and handicraft products, with direct sales to visitors. The release also presented BHARAT-TULIP as a way to market products through physical outlets and e-commerce platforms. The earlier Mysuru edition had concluded on October 19.[^5]

The design addresses a real gap: market access is more than putting a seller online. A small producer may need help with product presentation, customer contact, scheme awareness, and a first route to demand. A physical fair can create that first interaction while an online channel can, in principle, extend it beyond the event and locality. That is the right kind of cross-layer thinking—linking livelihoods, commerce, and digital distribution rather than treating onboarding as an outcome.

Still, the release does not name the e-commerce platforms involved or report online listings, orders, repeat buyers, delivery arrangements, seller fees, or net earnings. Its figures describe planned participation, not sales impact. To assess whether BHARAT-TULIP becomes durable infrastructure, the public needs follow-up reporting on how many artisans were listed, where products could be bought, whether orders were fulfilled, and what sellers actually received after costs. A fair is a valuable market event; it is not proof of a functioning nationwide commerce channel.

## Trends and implications

- **Commerce is a chain, not an app.** Electronic origin data, navigation charts, transport capacity, stock movement, and product discovery solve different failures. A marketplace cannot compensate for a shipment that is unpredictable or a seller who cannot prove eligibility for a trade benefit.
- **The week’s strongest digital story is interoperability between institutions.** The India–New Zealand MoC links customs administrations, while PAIMANA joins sector data in a public view. Both need clear standards, update schedules, and ways to challenge inaccurate records.
- **Access claims need outcome data.** Seller counts, roads awarded, chart coverage, cargo handled, and tonnes dispatched are inputs or outputs. They do not by themselves establish lower costs, timely delivery, higher earnings, or better consumer prices.
- **No new ONDC or GeM operating change is established by these developments.** Their relevance is adjacent: they show the public logistics, trade-document, and market-access layers on which digital commerce depends. Treating every e-commerce reference as a network integration would overstate the evidence.

## What to watch next

1. A technical implementation and outcome report for the India–New Zealand origin-data exchange.
2. PAIMANA’s source definitions and regional service-quality data, not only aggregate capacity and cargo.
3. Public, dated results for Kanda Express arrivals and local market prices, and for BHARAT-TULIP listings, orders, fulfilment, and seller receipts.
