Layer 6 — Governance & Grievance — spent the week publishing its own report cards and setting its own deadlines. On September 25, DARPG dropped the twin August scorecards: central ministries redressed 2,42,391 grievances at a 13-day average disposal time, while the states received 98,997 cases but redressed only 79,639, pushing portal-wide state pendency to 2,53,040 — a number that has grown roughly 42% since January. [^1] [^2] The annual pendency-clearing machine, Special Campaign 6.0, sat in its preparatory phase all week with grievance redressal written into its charter. [^3] On September 20, the Common Services Centres network held its CSC-CPGRAMS Grievance Day, advertising assisted complaint filing at ₹50 a complaint. [^4] And on September 23, Assam announced that every state government office goes paperless on e-Office by January 1, 2027. [^5] The pattern across all four: India's grievance layer has become exceptionally good at measuring disposal velocity — and remarkably quiet about everything a citizen actually experiences after filing.

## 1. The August Scorecards: Velocity Up, Queue Lengthening

The 52nd CPGRAMS monthly report for Central Ministries is, on its face, a triumph of reform. August saw 2,51,147 grievances received and 2,42,391 redressed; the Central Secretariat has now crossed one lakh monthly disposals for 50 consecutive months; and the average disposal time for 2026 stands at 13 days, well inside the 21-day benchmark that anchors the 10-step CPGRAMS reform process. [^1] The Grievance Redressal Assessment and Index crowned the Department of Posts, Department of Telecommunications and DFS (Insurance Division) in Group A, with Steel, Shipping and the North Eastern Ministry topping Group B. Nearly half of all grievances flowed to just three addresses — Rural Development (39,432), Labour and Employment (39,188) and DFS Banking Division (31,163) — confirming that India's complaint volume is fundamentally about welfare delivery and money. [^1]

The states tell the opposite story. The 49th report shows 98,997 PG cases received against 79,639 redressed — a net accretion of roughly 19,400 cases in a single month — with Uttar Pradesh alone accounting for 28,939 disposals, more than a third of the national total, ahead of Gujarat (6,810) and Rajasthan (5,895). [^2] Total state pendency stood at 2,53,040 on August 31, with 23 states and UTs carrying more than 1,000 pending cases each. Track the curve across the year's reports and the direction is unmistakable: 1,78,674 pending in January, 2,13,190 in May, 2,16,032 in June, 2,53,040 in August. [^6] [^7] [^8] Citizen demand is accelerating faster than state redressal capacity — new registrations hit 1,38,801 in August, the highest of 2026, up from 67,728 in January — and the backlog is the residue. [^6]

Two shadow metrics deserve more attention than they got. First, appeals: 24,085 came in and 24,611 were disposed in August, but appeal pendency stands at 16,693 — the second-tier remedy, the one that corrects bad first-tier disposals, is itself accumulating a queue. [^1] Second, satisfaction: the release is dense with velocity and volume figures but contains no citizen-satisfaction number at all. The last widely published figure came from the Ministry's 2024 year-end review — 44% satisfaction. [^9] When the scorecard stops printing the one metric that measures the citizen's experience rather than the bureaucracy's throughput, consumers should ask why. A 13-day average is a claim about the median file; satisfaction is a claim about whether the file's closure meant anything.

**The cross-layer angle:** DFS Banking Division's 31,163 grievances sit at the seam with Layer 2 — citizens routing payment and banking complaints through CPGRAMS alongside the RBI ombudsman, with no unified view of their complaint's lifecycle. And the silent satisfaction metric is a Layer 7 problem: without published outcome data, citizens cannot meaningfully contest "resolved" statuses.

## 2. Special Campaign 6.0: Grievance Redressal Enters Campaign Season

The preparatory phase of Special Campaign 6.0 ran through the entire week (September 16–30), ahead of the implementation window of October 2–31, bracketed by Gandhi Jayanti and Sardar Patel Jayanti. The monitoring portal Dr. Jitendra Singh launched on September 16 adds real-time tracking across preparatory, implementation and evaluation phases, with a dedicated e-waste module — this edition's theme is scientific waste management, e-waste disposal and pendency reduction. [^3] The campaign's five-year ledger is genuinely striking: ₹5,000 crore earned from scrap disposal, 1,000 lakh square feet of office space freed, around 2 crore files weeded, 24 lakh campaign sites. [^3]

Grievance redressal is written into the campaign's DNA — DARPG's campaign material this week tied the effort directly to CPGRAMS disposal, with the "Grievance Lodge" concept fronted by Samadhan Didi, the AI-enabled voice chatbot launched in May 2026 to take complaints by voice. [^8] Meanwhile the routine machinery kept its weekly rhythm beneath the campaign: 17,181 grievances disposed on September 22, 14,977 on September 23, 12,785 on September 24. [^10]

The consumer's stake is double-edged. Campaign-mode governance concentrates bureaucratic attention — pendency that idled for months gets cleared when a dashboard turns red — and the file-weeding exercise has a legitimate records-management rationale. But an October disposal push is also the month when "disposal" is most likely to be conflated with "redressal." A grievance closed as "settled" with a boilerplate reply satisfies the dashboard and not the complainant; the system's own feedback call centre exists precisely to catch this, yet satisfaction remains off the headline metrics. And weeding 2 crore files is record destruction as well as record hygiene — the campaign's cleanup deserves the same audit trail it demands of pending files, because deleted files are files that cannot be RTI'd later.

**The cross-layer angle:** Samadhan Didi puts a Layer 7 question inside a Layer 6 institution — voice complaints recorded, transcribed and classified by an AI agent raise DPDP questions about consent, retention and purpose limitation that the campaign material does not address.

## 3. Fifty Rupees to Complain: The CSC Channel's Access Price

On September 20, the CSC network observed its CPGRAMS Grievance Day, urging citizens to walk into a Common Services Centre and file complaints, with helpline 14599 for queries — at "only Rs. 50 per complaint." [^4] [^11] The fee is the Village Level Entrepreneur's service charge for assisted filing on the integrated CSC-CPGRAMS channel, which spans more than 5 lakh centres run by 2.5 lakh VLEs. [^1]

The August data shows how marginal — and how uneven — this last mile remains. Assisted filings through CSCs totalled 8,999, about 3.6% of the 2,51,147 grievances received on the portal. [^1] West Bengal dominates the channel, contributing 44% of all CSC-filed grievances; the same state also produced 42,054 of August's 1,38,801 new portal registrations — 30% of the national total. [^2] Whatever is driving West Bengal's grievance-surge (the reports don't say), it demonstrates that channel activation is highly localised: awareness campaigns, VLE incentives and state-level pushes move the numbers far more than the mere existence of 5 lakh access points.

The pricing deserves scrutiny on principle. Filing on CPGRAMS directly is free; the ₹50 buys assisted access — help with the form, the OTP, the category selection. That is a reasonable VLE business model and CSC entrepreneurship has genuinely extended state reach into villages. But it inverts the norm that complaining to the state costs nothing, and it prices access highest for the people least able to file online themselves — low-literacy, smartphone-less, elderly users in exactly the districts where grievances about pensions and welfare concentrate. Every other identity or entitlement rail in the DPI stack is free at the point of use; Layer 6's assisted channel is the outlier where the ticket to be heard carries a price. If assisted filing is public infrastructure, its economics should be published: what the ₹50 covers, what the VLE earns, and whether a zero-fee category for pensioners and welfare applicants is feasible.

**The cross-layer angle:** a VLE-assisted complaint means a third party mediates your grievance text — personal data passing through an intermediary that sits outside the citizen-to-state relationship DPDP assumes. Layer 6's last mile currently has no published data-handling rules for the intermediary that operates it.

## 4. Assam's Paperless Deadline: e-Office Gets a Date Certain

On September 23, Assam's Chief Minister's Office announced a hard date: every government office in the state moves to paperless e-Office functioning by January 1, 2027. [^5] This is expansion, not introduction — all 58 secretariat departments already run on the NIC-built platform, and implementation is extending to directorates and Deputy Commissioners' offices, with file creation, noting, digital signing, transfer tracking and archiving all going electronic. [^5]

For the grievance layer, e-Office is the substrate that makes everything else in this week's stories auditable. CPGRAMS timelines are enforceable because they are timestamped; the same logic applies to files themselves. A file that moves electronically leaves a trail — who held it, for how long, what they noted — which converts administrative delay from a citizen's vague grievance into a verifiable fact, discoverable through RTI and review meetings. Delhi's April restructuring of its eOffice into separate instances for departments, universities and local bodies showed the operational maturity such splits require; Assam's statewide deadline is a larger bet that the plumbing scales to districts. [^12]

The risks are the familiar ones of deadline-driven rollout — the same pattern Layer 5's AgriStack is running against September 30 procurement deadlines. No published readiness metrics accompany the announcement: connectivity in the Barak Valley and hill districts, availability of digital signature certificates, training depth beyond the secretariat, and — the quiet one — the fate of legacy paper files. A two-track administration, where pre-2027 paper lives in records rooms and post-2027 files live in e-Office, is a transparency regression disguised as modernisation unless the legacy digitisation plan is explicit. The announcement sets a deadline; the reform will be judged by whether citizens can eventually see file movement the way they can now see grievance status.

**The cross-layer angle:** e-Office depends on Layer 3's trust infrastructure — digital signatures and document standards — and its citizen-facing payoff depends on Layer 6's disclosure culture. Paperless files make delay visible only if someone is obligated to show the file.

## The Week's Throughline: Disposal Is Not Redressal

Layer 6's week was a parade of measurement: scorecards, GRAI rankings, daily disposal counts, campaign dashboards, paperless deadlines. What no artifact published: a satisfaction number, an appeal-quality audit, or a last mile that costs nothing. After last week's Delhi challan fight put the price of contesting a grievance on the front page, this week's reports show the same design gap systemwide — India has built world-class machinery for counting complaint closures and very little published machinery for auditing complaint outcomes. The pendency curve says demand is rising faster than the states can answer; the missing satisfaction metric says nobody outside the system is being asked whether the answers work.
