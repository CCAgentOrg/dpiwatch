# DPI Deep Dive — Tuesday | September 22, 2026

The week India's most successful public infrastructure finally got a price list, everyone with a stake in it went to war over the receipt. On September 15, NPCI issued the operational circular that converts last week's gazette notification into a working fee schedule: from October 15, person-to-merchant UPI payments above ₹2,000 will carry a 0.4% merchant discount rate, capped at ₹300 on transactions of ₹75,000 and above, while everything a consumer does free today — person-to-person transfers at any value, payments up to ₹2,000, QR payments at small vendors — stays free. [^1] [^2] What followed was seven days of escalating contestation: government sources insisting there is "no question of rollback," the opposition branding it the "UPI tax," the country's largest traders' body calling a national No-UPI Day, thousands of petrol pumps in one state preparing to refuse high-value UPI outright, and a Finance Ministry simultaneously denying American pressure and citing a parliamentary committee as the idea's parent. [^8] [^9] [^13] Beneath the noise sits a structural fact: the world's largest retail fast-payment system — 24.5 billion transactions worth roughly $315 billion in August alone — has been running on a budgetary subsidy the exchequer was already trimming, and this week the subsidy era formally began its end. [^20]

## 1. The Fine Print of the Fee

The circular itself was a committee product. On September 15, the UPI and Services Steering Committee met in Mumbai — about 22 representatives of banks and payment companies, chaired by NPCI chief executive Dilip Asbe, several flying in after Monday's gazette notification under the amended Section 10A of the Payment and Settlement Systems Act. [^3] The category map that emerged is more differentiated than the headlines suggested. The standard rate: 0.4% on specified person-to-merchant transactions above ₹2,000, capped at ₹300 for payments of ₹75,000 and above. Sector carve-outs: railways, telecom, insurance, fuel and agriculture-input payments above ₹2,000 attract a flat ₹5; capital-market transactions — equities, mutual funds — get a concessional 0.02%, also capped at ₹300. [^1] [^5] The exemptions are the shield: P2P transfers free at any value; transactions up to ₹2,000 on UPI and RuPay debit cards exempt; merchants receiving up to ₹1 lakh per month via UPI QR codes — the small-vendor backbone — pay nothing; credit-linked UPI (RuPay credit cards, pre-sanctioned credit lines) sits outside this framework entirely; and UPI apps are barred from levying any platform fee of their own. [^1] [^4] NPCI's same-day FAQ left no ambiguity on direction of payment: consumers pay nothing; the MDR is a charge within the payment ecosystem, distributed among banks and app providers. [^4]

The devolution formula matters as much as the rate: issuer banks get 40% of collections, acquiring banks 30%, UPI apps 20% — with 5% of everything collected seeding a new small-merchant acceptance-expansion fund targeted at Tier 3-6 cities, the Northeast, Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh, its design to be finalised with the RBI within three months. [^6] [^3] The fiscal context explains the timing. Budget 2026 estimated the UPI incentive payout at ₹2,000 crore, down from ₹2,196.21 crore actually paid in 2025-26 and ₹1,922.77 crore in 2024-25 — the fee replaces a subsidy that was already shrinking. [^7] The government's own exposure math is the political shield: only about 4% of merchant transactions will attract the charge, since P2P transfers alone constitute 70% of UPI's value. [^1] PhonePe's Sameer Nigam put the complementary figure on record — roughly 96% of receiving users remain exempt. [^9] The fine print, in other words, is genuinely protective. What it does not do is settle the two questions the rest of the week was about: whether merchants above the thresholds will swallow the fee or resist it, and who enforces the line where a merchant-side charge ends and a consumer price begins.

## 2. "Modi Tax" vs "Neither Tax Nor Cess": The Political War Over 40 Basis Points

The politics moved faster than the implementation. Within hours of the circular, Rahul Gandhi had branded the levy a "UPI tax" and demanded rollback; Congress president Mallikarjun Kharge and other opposition leaders joined; and the framing hardened into "Modi Tax" — with the sharpest version alleging the decision came under American pressure. [^9] [^8] Government sources shot back within a day: no question of a rethink, incentives for rural and semi-urban expansion will continue, and the large majority of payments stay free. [^8]

Then the fight moved to the committee record. BJP sources pointed to the August 2026 Parliamentary Standing Committee on Finance report, which recommended a calibrated, tiered revenue model for UPI and called for prompt notification — noting the panel included Congress members. Committee chairperson Bhartruhari Mahtab defended the report as unanimous: "everything on record." [^10] [^9] The Congress reply was surgical: MP Manish Tewari said the committee had only recommended that the government *explore* a self-reliant, tiered revenue model, and that the specific MDR proposal was never brought back before the panel — "explore is not support." [^11] The distinction is not pedantic; it is the difference between a direction and a rate card, and it went straight to the legitimacy question of who decided 0.4%.

Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman closed the week's argument on September 21, telling ANI the charge is "neither a tax nor a cess," that the funds will not enter the Consolidated Fund of India, that it is "a charge between operators," and that it "will not be passed on to the consumer" — while accusing the opposition of spreading misinformation. [^12] The consumer's honest ledger sits between the two camps. A levy on intermediaries that never reaches prices is not how fees behave; but a fee that funds acceptance expansion in underserved districts is not how taxes behave either. The accountability gap is the real issue: the old subsidy flowed through budgets, subject to parliamentary audit. MDR will flow through private intermediaries, subject to — so far — nothing public. The consumer ask is minimal and urgent: quarterly publication of MDR collections and deployment of the small-merchant fund, before October 15 sets the precedent that this revenue is nobody's to explain.

## 3. The Revolt Before the Fee: Fuel Pumps, Black Cloth, and No-UPI Day

The merchant response converted commentary into a calendar. In Madhya Pradesh, the state petrol pumps dealers association — about 4,700 fuel stations — announced it will stop accepting UPI payments above ₹2,000 for petrol and diesel from October 16 unless the MDR is withdrawn. Association president Ajay Singh put the arithmetic on record: at 0.4%, a ₹10,000 fill costs the pump ₹40, compounding to a claimed ₹17,700 monthly hit on already-thin margins — and fuel sits in the flat-₹5 category besides, a double squeeze the sector wants removed entirely. [^13] The Confederation of All India Traders scaled the resistance nationally: a "No-UPI Day" on September 23, with participating businesses refusing high-value UPI for the day and covering QR-code scanners and payment sound boxes in black cloth — Indore and Bhopal traders formalised it first, and CAIT is targeting over 10 lakh (one million) participants across states. [^14] [^15] CAIT's Odisha unit has separately raised the GST that applies atop the MDR and floated a boycott if relief doesn't come. [^9]

The industry's own response is stratified. Large merchants are signalling absorption: Speciality Restaurants (Oh! Calcutta, Mainland China) expects no pass-through in a competitive market, with annual UPI volumes of ₹7-8 crore. The Retailers Association of India's Kumar Rajagopalan named the real exposure: "Small merchants will now think if they should accept cash or UPI" — precisely the segments above ₹1 lakh monthly that the exemption doesn't cover, just ahead of the festive season when trader bodies warn buyers could drift back to cash. [^16] The government's counter-move is enforcement-shaped: in Delhi, the Finance Ministry has begun discussions with payment aggregators and stakeholders to sensitise them and ensure merchants do not charge customers — an acknowledgment that pass-through, not the fee itself, is the politically detonable part. [^17] Daily monitoring is to begin with implementation on October 15. [^9] This is the first large-scale consumer-choice counter-pressure a DPI rail has faced at this scale, and it arrives before the fee is even live — a stress test the rail's designers did not schedule but probably needed.

## 4. The USTR Undercurrent: What the MDR Fight Is Really About

Beneath the domestic shouting lies a quieter dispute about what the fee is *for*. The Department of Financial Services issued a point-by-point defense on September 17, calling the claim that MDR was introduced under external influence "patently false and misleading." The trigger was the US Trade Representative's 2026 National Trade Estimate report, which raised concerns that American electronic payment providers cannot participate in the UPI ecosystem — including credit transactions on UPI — on a level playing field with RuPay. [^18] The DFS response doubles as industrial policy, stated without embarrassment: "The NPCI circular of September 15, 2026, does not allow credit transactions on UPI by any other credit card other than the RuPay credit card. There is a clear policy of only allowing RuPay credit cards on UPI to enable RuPay credit cards to become the preferred choice of credit cards amongst users in India." [^17] [^18]

Read together, the two threads of the week connect: the MDR is the funding mechanism for a deliberately closed credit loop. A self-sustaining rail — one that no longer needs the exchequer's justification — can keep RuPay credit as the sole card instrument on UPI indefinitely, growing a domestic interchange economy inside a network foreign card schemes can't enter. That is sovereignty policy wearing a fee schedule, and it is entirely coherent with last week's GFF mandate that India stop paying foreign networks' tolls. But consumers should hold both ideas at once: the "no external pressure" claim and the "RuPay-only by design" claim are both true, which means the US-pressure allegation was always the wrong argument — the real one is whether a publicly seeded rail should harden into a closed national champion without a published roadmap for third-party instruments. UPI's legitimacy was built on openness. The credit loop is where that legacy is now being spent.

## 5. The Borders Widen While the Fee Lands

Even as the fee fight raged at home, the export machine kept moving. On September 16, NPCI International's Sonal Asnani briefed foreign journalists in Mumbai on UPI Global Acceptance: the scheme now works with 11 countries on international merchant acceptance, Kazakhstan is studying cross-border QR payments with UPI as the explicit "blueprint," and — in a detail worth codifying — users scanning a participating local QR see the transaction amount, exchange rate and applicable fees *before* confirming. [^19] By September 21, NIPL's presentation in the UAE put numbers on the machine being exported: UPI processed 24.5 billion transactions worth around $315 billion in August, roughly 80% of India's digital payments now run on it, and more than 500 million people actively use it — with the UAE featuring in both the UPI acceptance column and the RuPay card acceptance column. [^20]

The connection to this week's fee is not rhetorical. A rail that charges merchants at home can present itself abroad as self-sustaining infrastructure rather than subsidised diplomacy — the "digital public good" pitch now carries a price list, which makes the cross-border pricing disclosures (that pre-transaction FX-and-fees visibility) the single most important consumer protection to hold every new corridor to. The distinction that matters in reading these announcements is acceptance versus linkage: QR acceptance through a local acquirer serves inbound tourists and scales fast; the remittance-cost reduction that anchors the government's global pitch needs the harder bilateral linkage work. August's $315 billion scoreboard makes UPI the strongest negotiating position in global retail payments. The MDR week showed the harder half of that position: the world is watching how the rail prices itself at home before trusting what it charges abroad.

## The Week's Ledger

October 15 is 23 days away, and September 23 — No-UPI Day — is tomorrow's stress test. Watch five things: CAIT's actual turnout and whether the protest generalises beyond MP; whether the petrol-pump defection holds past its October 16 deadline; pass-through enforcement, starting with Delhi's aggregator discussions and the promised daily monitoring; the RBI consultation that must design the small-merchant fund within three months; and the Standing Committee follow-up, where "explore is not support" will meet the notification record. The week proved the fee was the easy part. Pricing a public good is an accounting exercise; contending over it is the politics of a utility — and India's payment rail just became one, formally, in both senses. The consumer's statutory shield below ₹2,000 is real and unprecedented. Above that line, "the merchant pays" is a claim that will be tested at checkout counters, not in press releases — and everyone from the Finance Minister to a petrol pump in Bhopal knows it.
