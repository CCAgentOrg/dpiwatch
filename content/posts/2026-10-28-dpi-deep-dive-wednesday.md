# DPI Deep Dive — Wednesday | October 28, 2026

**Focus layer:** L3 — Documents & Data Exchange (DigiLocker, API Setu and eSign)  
**Coverage period:** 21–28 October 2026; checked through 08:30 IST on 28 October

## Executive summary

This week the documents layer was tested at both ends of its trust chain. At the trust end, a researcher's disclosure that eight eMudhra intermediate certificate-authority keys could be reconstructed from public data set off same-day browser blocks, a company exit from the global TLS certificate business, a 36% two-day share fall and a MeitY investigation team — alongside MeitY's reassurance that India's CCA-regulated digital signature and eSign services remain unaffected. At the citizen end, DigiLocker announced that all 309 urban local bodies in Rajasthan are now live on the platform, while explainers circulated clarifying that DigiLocker cannot change the mobile number or email registered against a person's Aadhaar. The week's lesson is that a documents layer is only as strong as the key ceremonies that sign it, the issuing databases behind it, and the clarity of what each interface can and cannot do.

## Key developments

### 1. Eight weak CA keys, revoked in four days — after eighteen months in the wild

The core technical facts are now well documented across incident trackers and security press. On 17 October 2026, an independent researcher credited as "Wayne" reported that the private keys of eight eMudhra intermediate CA certificates — P-384 keys in the company's emSign G3 hierarchy — were so weak they could be recovered from the public certificates in under a second. One of the eight keys was, quite literally, the number 4. As proof, the researcher included a signed attestation generated with each recovered key. Mozilla and Chrome blocked the intermediates the same day; eMudhra revoked all eight on 18 October; Apple blocked them on 19 October and Microsoft on 20 October, when a public incident site maintained by Mozilla's Dennis Jackson went live. [Keytos's technical timeline](https://www.keytos.io/blog/pki/emudhra-ca-key-compromise) and [ArmorCode's write-up](https://www.armorcode.com/blog/emudhra-ca-keys-compromised) trace the lineage further back: the weak keys were generated at a key ceremony around April 2025, and an early warning sign — eMudhra publishing certificate revocation lists with invalid signatures for the same intermediates in May 2025 — went unexamined for roughly seventeen months. That asymmetry deserves emphasis: remediation took four days once disclosed, but the weak keys sat in live, publicly trusted intermediates for about eighteen months.

CERT-In's advisory attributed the weakness to key generation with insufficient randomness — the values were so small they could be recovered almost instantly from their public counterparts, as [The New Indian Express reported](https://www.newindianexpress.com/lifestyle/tech/2026/Oct/22/how-a-flaw-in-emudhras-digital-keys-raised-concerns-over-internet-security). The failure was a process failure, not an intrusion, and that distinction shapes everything downstream. The blast radius extended well past browsers: operating systems, language runtimes and package managers that bundle trust stores had to ship fixes, including NSS 3.131.1, Python certifi 2026.10.19 and pip 26.2.2, alongside advisories for Go ([CVE-2026-111943](https://socradar.io/blog/emudhra-ca-key-compromise-weak-keys-risks-and-fixes)) and Firefox (CVE-2026-113470). A command-line tool or application carrying its own CA bundle was not automatically protected by browser updates — a reminder that trust-store hygiene is a supply-chain problem, not just a settings panel.

For the documents layer specifically, the incident is a stress test of the infrastructure that makes eSign and digital signature certificates verifiable. Signing works because keys are generated, stored and audited under the Controller of Certifying Authorities' regime. Eight keys that any attacker could derive mean that, for roughly eighteen months, an attacker in possession of the public certificates could have impersonated those intermediaries within the affected hierarchy. The browser-root programs caught it — but through an outside researcher, not through the CA's own controls. That is the governance question the rest of this edition returns to.

### 2. "Unaffected" versus "compromised": the definitional gap inside MeitY's reassurance

On 21 October, MeitY said digital signature certificates and eSign services regulated by the Controller of Certifying Authorities remain unaffected by the eMudhra incident, that the reported security event concerned eMudhra's global certificate business, and that it had constituted a special team to investigate. The ministry also cautioned against social media accounts spreading fear about the impact. [The Times of India carried the statement](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/business/india-business/emudhra-breach-it-ministry-says-digital-signature-esign-services-unaffected-sets-up-probe-team/articleshow/135094581.cms); [The Week reported](https://www.theweek.in/news/biz-tech/2026/10/22/why-did-emudhra-shares-tank-today-are-your-dscs-safe.html) that eMudhra's shares fell more than 36% across two consecutive sessions to ₹345, that the company says Indian DSC and eSign services are running normally, and that the affected global TLS business contributes only around 1% of revenue. eMudhra has said it will withdraw from the global SSL/TLS certificate business following the incident, and is working with affected customers on replacement certificates.

Both the reassurance and the alarm can be literally true at once, because the word "compromised" is doing different work in different statements. CERT-In treats weak, recoverable keys as compromised material; eMudhra uses "compromise" to mean an intrusion into its systems, which it denies. [Incident trackers have noted](https://shattered.io/emudhra-exits-global-tls-certificate-business-2026) that the two formulations sit uneasily together, and the likeliest reconciliation is definitional. For the public, though, the takeaway differs sharply depending on which definition one assumes — and that is precisely why regulators should standardise incident vocabulary for trust services.

The substantive question for the L3 layer is narrower and answerable: Indian eSign and DSC issuance run on a separate, CCA-supervised hierarchy from the revoked global intermediates, so MeitY's statement is plausible as written. But the probe team's remit should reach past the global business to the systemic issue — the same company produced eight catastrophically weak keys through its key-generation process, and an internal red flag (invalid CRL signatures) went unaddressed. What would make the investigation credible rather than reassuring: publication of its scope and findings, confirmation that key ceremonies on the CCA-supervised Indian hierarchy are being re-audited with the same technical rigour, CCA's own audit history of eMudhra as a licensed Certifying Authority and eSign Service Provider, and whether issuance controls will add technical validation that generated keys meet strength requirements. Consumers have little to act on for Indian eSign — signatures remain verifiable — but administrators should still check whether any service they run trusts emSign global roots, and update bundled trust stores.

### 3. Rajasthan's 309 urban local bodies go live on DigiLocker — reach is not correctness

DigiLocker announced this week, via its official channels, that all 309 Urban Local Bodies in Rajasthan — 10 Municipal Corporations, 49 Municipal Councils and 250 Municipal Boards — are now live on the platform, letting residents fetch civic documents digitally instead of visiting an office for paper copies. [The announcement](https://x.com/digilocker_ind/status/2109579258800808385) makes Rajasthan one of the deeper state integrations on the platform, which now lists 125+ issuers for the state on its [issuers directory](https://www.digilocker.gov.in). The scale context, though dated, is worth keeping beside the headline: a Parliament reply reported on 12 August 2026 put DigiLocker at [72.43 crore registered users, 72.86 crore document transactions over three years and 5,437 document types](https://pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2298116), and a PIB factsheet counted more than 934 crore documents issued as of 31 July 2026.

Municipal documents are the last mile of this layer in the most literal sense. Birth and death certificates gate school admissions, passports, insurance claims and inheritance; trade licences gate livelihoods. If the integration works as intended, it removes queue-and-intermediary friction for exactly the households that can least afford it. But integration is a reach metric, not a quality metric. DigiLocker fetches whatever the municipal database holds; it does not clean it. A wrong name, date or parentage entry fetched digitally arrives faster, with a digital seal, and is arguably harder to challenge at the counter than a paper record the clerk can visibly re-check. The governance ask that should accompany every "N bodies live" announcement: per-issuer measures of record accuracy and update latency, a published grievance route for wrong records fetched through the platform, and clarity on which document classes are "issued" into DigiLocker by the municipal system — legally at par with physical originals — versus merely uploaded by users. A fetch counter tells us the pipe exists; it does not tell us the water is clean.

### 4. What DigiLocker cannot do: the Aadhaar-update confusion and the read-mostly design

A second strand of the week's coverage clarifies the layer's boundary. Explain­ers in [Mint](https://www.livemint.com/news/india/aadhaar-update-2026-can-you-change-your-mobile-number-or-email-id-via-digilocker-heres-the-answer-11793106798432.html), [Mashable](https://in.mashable.com/tech/115376/aadhaar-update-2026-can-you-change-your-mobile-number-email-id-through-digilocker-details-here) and [The CSR Journal](https://thecsrjournal.in/digilocker-users-cannot-change-aadhaar-mobile-number-or-email-id-updates-must-be-done-at-designated-centres-confirms-government) on 27–28 October relayed the government's clarification that DigiLocker users cannot change their Aadhaar-registered mobile number or email through the app; those updates require UIDAI's own process, including designated centres. Separately, education-focused coverage this week highlighted the issued-versus-uploaded distinction that matters at admission time: an issuer-provided DigiLocker marksheet is a digitally signed document from the source system, while a self-uploaded scan is neither verified nor legally at par with the original.

The confusion is predictable from the design, and the design is correct. DigiLocker is a read-mostly window over issuer databases: each record's source of truth remains the issuing authority — UIDAI for Aadhaar, a municipal body for a birth certificate, a board for a marksheet — and writes must happen there. One writable copy per record is the right security architecture. But it produces a perception gap: "my documents app" is not "my documents office". Citizens who treat DigiLocker as the front door for all document life will keep discovering, one dead end at a time, that the app can display a record but not repair it. The inexpensive fix is in-app signposting: when a user opens a document class they cannot alter, DigiLocker could route them to the issuer's actual correction path — the UIDAI update flow, the municipal office, the board — with the document type pre-identified. That converts the boundary from a frustration into a map. It also connects the layers: the correction journey for an Aadhaar-linked document is an L1 (UIDAI) journey reached through an L3 (DigiLocker) interface, and the citizen experiences both as one system.

## Trends & implications

- **Trust infrastructure is process infrastructure.** Eight weak keys were a ceremony failure, flagged internally by bad CRL signatures seventeen months before disclosure. Publishing the probe team's findings matters more than any reassurance adjective.
- **Definitions do public work.** "Compromised" means different things to CERT-In and to the company whose keys are in question. Standardised incident vocabulary for trust services would prevent reassurance and alarm from being simultaneously true.
- **Integration counters are not quality counters.** 309 ULBs live is a reach milestone. Accuracy, update latency and grievance routes are the metrics citizens actually experience, and they should be published per issuer.
- **Read-mostly by design needs a map.** DigiLocker's refusal to write to issuer records is sound; in-app routing to each issuer's correction path would keep that boundary from feeling like a dead end.

## Upcoming milestones

1. MeitY's special investigation team: published scope, timeline and findings, including whether key ceremonies on the CCA-supervised Indian hierarchy are being re-audited.
2. Root-program completion: tracking reports put Chrome's removal of the emSign ECC Root CA – G3 root at 27 October; the remaining cleanups across OS and runtime trust stores should be confirmed in vendor release notes.
3. eMudhra's replacement-certificate programme for affected global TLS customers, and any CCA or sectoral regulator advisories touching eSign-dependent workflows.
4. Rajasthan's ULB integration: whether issued (not uploaded) municipal certificates flow through DigiLocker, and whether per-ULB data-quality reporting follows the launch.
5. Whether DigiLocker adds issuer-correction signposting, turning the read-mostly boundary into an explicit route map for citizens.
