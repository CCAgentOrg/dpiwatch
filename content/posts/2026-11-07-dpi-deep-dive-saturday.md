# DPI Deep Dive — Saturday | November 07, 2026

**Focus Layer:** L6 — Governance & Grievance: DARPG, CPGRAMS, eOffice and the administrative backbone  
**Coverage Period:** October 31 – November 7, 2026

## Executive summary

This week the grievance layer was written into statute for the country's largest workfare programme, even as the campaign machinery that measures grievance disposal wrapped up its annual scorecard. The Rural Development Ministry notified the VB-G RAM G Grievance Redressal Rules, 2026 on November 2 — a three-tier, digitally tracked system with a 15-day resolution clock — but a draft-to-final comparison shows the accountability teeth (weekly grievance days, penalties for delay, FIR provisions) were left in the May draft. Special Campaign 6's results rolled in from DPIIT, the Environment Ministry and Indian Railways, reporting near-total grievance disposal alongside 45 lakh files weeded under e-Office 7.0. CPGRAMS published its usual daily disposal ledgers — roughly 37,400 central grievances in four disclosed days — and, notably, celebrated resolving a private telecom network complaint. Bihar ordered a paperless, AI-assisted e-Cabinet, extending the NIC's eOffice lineage to the state's highest decision-making body, and the Pension Ministry launched its biggest-ever Digital Life Certificate campaign across 2,000 cities. The week's throughline: India's grievance infrastructure is now superb at registering, tracking and disposing — and the contested frontier is what disposal actually delivers: relief, accountability, and published data.

## Key developments

### 1. VB-G RAM G gets its grievance rules — with the accountability clauses left on the cutting room floor

On November 2, the Union Ministry of Rural Development notified the Grievance Redressal Rules, 2026 under the Viksit Bharat – Guarantee for Rozgar and Ajeevika Mission (Gramin) Act, 2025 — the statute that replaced MGNREGA. The rules mandate a three-tier grievance redressal mechanism at gram panchayat, block and district levels, with designated grievance redressal officers at each level responsible for receiving, registering, inquiring into and disposing of complaints, and the District Programme Coordinator overseeing the district tier.[^1][^2]

The procedural architecture is genuinely citizen-facing. Complaints can be filed in person, by speed post, on the designated digital platform or via telephone helplines; a complaint received physically must be entered on the digital platform no later than the next working day; and no complaint can be rejected solely because of its language, format or manner of submission. Enquiries must be completed within seven working days and complaints resolved within 15 days, with unresolved cases escalating automatically to the next higher level and remaining tracked until disposal. Appeals follow the Act's Schedule I provisions, with a 45-day filing window and a 30-day disposal deadline. Section 25(2) of the parent Act spells out what workers can complain about: denial of employment, delayed or unpaid wages, non-payment of unemployment allowance, inadequate worksite facilities, discrimination and harassment.[^3]

But the story with the longest tail is what did not survive the drafting process. Comparing the May 22 draft with the final notification, Business Standard documented the omissions: the draft's express mandate for a **weekly grievance day** for workers is gone; provisions for **penal action against officials for delays** have not been retained; the clause requiring an **FIR in cases of financial irregularities** is omitted; and mandates for **panchayat reviews and quarterly reports** to state governments on complaints received, resolved and pending are no longer expressly required.[^3]

The nuance matters. These safeguards are not merely draft-stage ideas — paragraph 28 of Schedule I of the VB-G RAM G Act itself already provides for accountability machinery, and rules as subordinate legislation cannot override statutory obligations. So the omissions do not strip workers of their rights under the Act; what they remove is *clarity*: the implementing rules no longer specify how weekly grievance days will run, who faces consequences for delay, or what reporting cadence makes complaint data visible above the block. The burden shifts from rule to Act to interpretation — and, in practice, onto the worker who must invoke it.[^3] With social audit at panchayat level and technology-based monitoring mandated elsewhere in the scheme's design, the test for this layer is whether the digital tracking platform publishes what it tracks. A grievance system that counts escalations but hides outcomes converts a rights guarantee into a ticketing queue.

### 2. Special Campaign 6's scorecards land: 99% disposal, 45 lakh files weeded, and the disposal metric under the spotlight

DARPG's Special Campaign 6 — the annual swachhata-and-pendency drive running October 2–31 — concluded just before this window opened, and the ministry-by-ministry results were published through the week. DPIIT reported on November 2 that it disposed of over 99% of public grievances recorded under the campaign and closed roughly 83% of identified electronic files.[^4] The Environment Ministry closed its campaign at 132 locations with 449 of 450 targeted grievances disposed (99.78%), all 96 appeals cleared, ₹64.18 lakh in scrap revenue and 32,854 sq ft of space freed — while also reporting that only 29 of 37 MP references and 8 of 16 pending parliamentary assurances were cleared.[^5] The Fisheries and Land Resources departments reported 100% grievance and appeal disposal.[^4]

The Railways numbers deserve their own read. As of October 29, Indian Railways had disposed 94% of pending public grievances on CPGRAMS against a target of 80%, cleared 1,200 files pending more than three years, weeded out 45 lakh physical files, and digitised records under **e-Office 7.0** — the file-management system that is the layer's true back office. Its Rail Jan Samvaad exercise on October 22 took passenger feedback at 5,884 stations in a single day.[^6] Mid-campaign, the government-wide tally stood at ₹491.28 crore from scrap disposal across 8,34,509 cleanliness sites.[^7]

The analysis point is in the metric itself. Campaign reporting measures *disposal* — the closure rate against a target uploaded to the SCDPM portal. It does not measure whether the grievant agreed with the closure, whether "disposed" meant relief or a template reply, or whether e-file closure preserved anything auditable. When a ministry can hit 99.78% on grievances while clearing half its parliamentary assurances, the campaign numbers reveal the hierarchy of internal incentives: grievance disposal is the score that counts. DARPG's own monthly reporting shows central ministries averaged 13 days disposal time in 2026 — speed is real.[^15] The next maturity step for this layer is publishing *quality* telemetry alongside speed: feedback ratings, appeal overturn rates, and category-level outcome data that citizens can actually audit.

### 3. CPGRAMS' week in numbers — and a telecom success story that says something larger

DARPG's daily disposal ledgers ran all week. On November 2, CPGRAMS recorded 8,751 grievances and 1,746 appeals disposed by central ministries; November 3 saw 10,361 grievances and 1,753 appeals; November 5 logged 9,472 grievances and 2,199 appeals; and November 6, 8,818 grievances and 1,593 appeals — roughly 37,400 central grievances and 7,300 appeals across the four disclosed days, plus 4,482 state/UT disposals reported on November 4.[^8] On November 2, the department also publicised that grievances can now be lodged by voice or text and tracked in **22 official languages** — the multilingual front-end that Bhashini-era grievance design has been building toward.[^9]

The more revealing post came on November 5: a success story titled "CPGRAMS Facilitates Airtel Network Issue Resolution," celebrating the redressal of a citizen's complaint about a private telecom operator's network in Uttar Pradesh through the government portal.[^10] This is worth pausing on. CPGRAMS' statutory design is for grievances against public authorities on service delivery; private telecom complaints formally belong to the operator's complaint-escalation ladder under TRAI's telecom consumer protection framework. A government grievance portal pitching itself as the effective route to fix a private company's network is either a sign of pragmatic state capacity — the ministry routes the complaint, the operator responds because a central portal asked — or a symptom that sectoral ombudsman channels are not working well enough to be the first stop. Probably both. The consumer-protection implication: when the government portal outperforms the sector's own redress ladder, that is data TRAI should be studying, and it is exactly the kind of cross-layer signal (governance meets telecom) that only becomes visible when a portal publishes its own success stories with specificity.

For context on scale: DARPG's September report for states/UTs recorded 99,134 grievances received and 1,15,168 redressed — the year's highest disposal — while CPGRAMS' integration with over 5 lakh Common Service Centres channelled 12,996 grievances through CSCs that month, with Karnataka alone contributing 54% of CSC-filed grievances.[^16] Access infrastructure exists; the distribution of who actually uses it remains heavily skewed.

### 4. Bihar orders a paperless, AI-assisted e-Cabinet — the eOffice idea reaches the cabinet table

On November 4, Bihar Chief Minister Samrat Choudhary chaired a review of the state's e-Cabinet system at Sankalp Auditorium in Patna, directing officials to roll it out soon so that every cabinet file, agenda and decision moves onto a digital platform. The Cabinet Secretariat Department's Additional Chief Secretary Arvind Kumar Choudhary presented the system, which NIC Bihar developed and demonstrated to the Chief Minister; the Chief Minister emphasised using AI and digital tools to make cabinet workflows "modern, transparent, technology-driven and paperless." No go-live date was announced.[^11][^12]

The governance-layer significance is architectural rather than anecdotal. eOffice — NIC's file-management workflow that the Centre has rolled out across ministries (and that Railways is digitising under version 7.0) — is designed for departmental files. A cabinet is a different order of system: the highest-stakes deliberative process in a state, with politically sensitive notes, and the least public scrutiny of any government record. Digitising it promises real gains — version control, audit trails, faster circulation, searchable precedent — and Bihar's move follows a family of e-Cabinet deployments NIC has built for other states and the centre's own system. But "transparent" is doing a lot of work in the Chief Minister's formulation: a digital cabinet is not an open cabinet. The machine-readable cabinet decision, however, creates the *technical precondition* for future disclosure — decisions that could be published, differences between drafts and finals that could be surfaced. Whether Bihar's e-Cabinet eventually publishes what it processes is the metric to watch; the software will not decide that, the government will.[^11]

### 5. Jeevan Pramaan Campaign 5: the annual proof-of-life ritual goes national at maximum scale

On November 1, the Department of Pension and Pensioners' Welfare — DARPG's sibling in the Ministry of Personnel, Public Grievances and Pensions — launched its fifth and biggest-ever nationwide Digital Life Certificate campaign, running November 1–30 across 2,000 cities and districts. The campaign's technology centrepiece is Aadhaar-based **face authentication**: pensioners need only an Android smartphone, the AadhaarFaceRD and Jeevan Pramaan Face apps, and a working camera — no biometric fingerprint device. Pensioners aged 80+ got an early window from October 1; everyone else must submit by November 30 to keep pensions flowing without interruption.[^13] As of the first days of November, the Jeevan Pramaan portal's counter showed over 17,500 certificates submitted since November 1 — early days in a month-long campaign targeting lakhs of pensioners.[^14]

This is the governance layer's most intimate DPI ritual: an annual, state-mandated proof that a citizen is still alive, executed through the identity layer. The face-authentication push is a genuine accessibility win — it removes the fingerprint-scan failures that plague elderly manual labourers and the travel burden on immobile pensioners, with doorstep banking as a fallback. But it also concentrates a pensioner's entire income continuity on one authentication event against one biometric database. A failed face match, an Aadhaar-mobile linkage that has lapsed, or a phone-less household converts directly into a stopped pension until the failure is diagnosed. The campaign model implicitly acknowledges this by keeping physical submission at bank branches open. The design question the layer should keep answering publicly: what fraction of campaign-era submissions fail authentication, and how fast are failures resolved? Disbursement data would show it; publication remains the discretionary part.

## Cross-layer connections

- **Grievance design meets workfare identity (L5→L6):** the VB-G RAM G rules inherit the same edge-case problem last week's AgriStack stories exposed — entitlements that live in databases, with exclusion producing no rejection letter. A 15-day disposal clock only helps workers who are visible in the system; the notification's silence on how the digital platform will publish outcomes echoes the Farmer Registry's silence on composition.
- **eOffice is the connective tissue (L6 internal):** Railways' 45-lakh-file e-Office 7.0 digitisation, DPIIT's e-file closures, and Bihar's e-Cabinet are three tiers of the same NIC stack — departmental files, ministry pendency, and cabinet deliberation. The audit-and-disclosure questions scale with sensitivity up that stack.
- **Governance portal as consumer redress (L6→L7/L2):** CPGRAMS celebrating an Airtel fix blurs the boundary between public grievance systems and TRAI's sectoral consumer protection — worth watching as a de facto expansion of the portal's mandate.
- **Identity layer carrying welfare continuity (L1→L6):** Jeevan Pramaan's face-authentication dependence ties pension continuity to Aadhaar's biometric quality — the clearest monthly demonstration of identity DPI's downstream stakes.

## What to watch next

1. Whether states operationalising VB-G RAM G grievance rules re-introduce weekly grievance days in their own implementation orders, and whether MoRD's digital platform for the scheme publishes complaint-outcome data at district level.
2. DARPG's campaign-consolidation report for Special Campaign 6 — and whether the department adds satisfaction or appeal-overturn metrics to next year's targets.
3. Bihar's e-Cabinet go-live date, and whether any cabinet decisions or agendas are published in machine-readable form.
4. Jeevan Pramaan's final November tally versus last year's campaign, and any published data on face-authentication failure rates among 80+ pensioners.
5. Whether CPGRAMS success stories about private companies become a pattern — and if TRAI's consumer protection apparatus responds.
