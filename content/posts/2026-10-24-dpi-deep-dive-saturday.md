# DPI Deep Dive — Saturday | October 24, 2026

**Focus layer:** L6 — Governance & Grievance: administrative records, public-service complaints, and the systems used to monitor or manage people and places.

**Coverage period:** October 17–24, 2026.

## Executive summary

This week’s governance developments are less about a newly announced national grievance portal than about the machinery behind public administration: a campaign scoreboard combining grievance and file-clearance targets; a proposed in-person and online redress drive for women’s scheme beneficiaries; and two award-winning uses of data for crowd safety and village planning. The official releases report substantial activity, but they do not offer independent outcome audits. Their central distinction is between *administrative completion*—a case marked addressed, a file reviewed, a camera response coordinated—and a remedy that the affected person can verify, challenge, and trust.

The strongest cross-layer signal is that governance DPI is not just CPGRAMS. It includes the local channels through which complaints are heard, the digital records officials use to act, and the analytics applied to beneficiaries, villages, or public spaces. Each needs a visible human route when the data or the decision is wrong.

## Key developments

### 1. Special Campaign 6 puts grievance targets beside the government’s file backlog

Several departments published mid-campaign results for Special Campaign 6, which runs from 2 to 31 October. The Department of Posts reported that, by 16 October, it had resolved 100,214 public grievances against a target of 150,000 and disposed of 764 appeals against 1,500. The Department of Land Resources said 90% of its campaign grievance target had been disposed of; it also reported reviewing 945 of 1,280 physical files and 242 of 501 e-files. The Water Resources Department’s 23 October release gave a more granular table as of 19 October: 161 of 190 public grievances (84.7% of its target) and 37 of 61 appeals (60.6%); it had reviewed 3,460 of 6,443 e-files and closed 835. The Food and Public Distribution Department, reporting after three weeks, said it had addressed 1,030 grievances and 161 appeals, reviewed 14,663 e-files and closed 7,331.[^1][^2][^3][^4]

These figures are useful as workload disclosures, but not as a common performance league table. Departments set different targets and report at different cut-off dates. “Resolved,” “disposed,” and “addressed” are not defined consistently in these releases, and no denominator for all grievances received is supplied. A percentage of a campaign target is not a resolution rate for every complaint filed. E-file reviews and closures matter to the back office, but cannot be added to grievance disposals or treated as proof that a citizen’s issue was fixed.

The more useful next release would link each department’s target to its opening backlog, new cases received, cases actually resolved, transfers, rejected or reopened cases, appeal outcomes, and age of pending work. That would let the public distinguish a short-term campaign push from durable capacity. Digital records can make a decision easier to find and audit; the value depends on whether files remain searchable and their contents can be corrected after a mistake.

### 2. DigiPost uses the postal network as a physical last mile for a digital notice

The Department of Posts’ same 18 October report also announced DigiPost, described as a “phygital” mail service: traffic e-challans issued by Gujarat Police are transmitted digitally for physical delivery to citizens. That is a modest but important design choice. A digital government record can travel through a delivery network that reaches people who may not regularly check an app, maintain a stable data connection, or recognise a message from an unfamiliar sender. It links digital administration to a real-world delivery layer rather than assuming the citizen is permanently online.[^1]

The release does not say how many notices have been sent, how quickly delivery occurs, how undelivered notices are handled, or how a recipient verifies that a challan is genuine. Nor does it explain how receipt and appeal deadlines work if a postal delivery is delayed or the address is wrong. Those details are central to procedural fairness: physical delivery should widen access to notice, not create another opaque hand-off between police and post. A public service standard covering delivery time, returned mail, authenticity checks, and an accessible dispute route would make DigiPost more than a promising channel announcement.

### 3. Poshan Tracker and PMMVY grievances get a planned “chaupal” route

On 21 October, the Ministry of Women and Child Development said it was planning a nationwide one-day drive for 30 October under Special Campaign 6. The plan asks field institutions to address grievances received through Poshan Tracker and the Pradhan Mantri Matru Vandana Yojana (PMMVY) by that date. For cases still pending, designated Grievance Redressal Officers are to organise Grievance Redressal Chaupals or e-Chaupals. The ministry said 94 officers from States and Union Territories joined the planning meeting; it also asked field institutions to document activities and upload them to the Ministry’s mission portals.[^5]

This is a useful recognition that sectoral apps do not automatically create a complete remedy. A beneficiary may need help with a missing benefit, a status that does not match events on the ground, or a complaint that has stalled inside a scheme workflow. A local chaupal can bring a responsible officer and the complainant into the same process; an e-chaupal may extend that process where travel is difficult. The announcement is a plan, not evidence that the drive has happened or that backlogs were cleared. It provides no baseline count of grievances, response-time standard, location list, or method for confirming that the complainant accepted the resolution.

The test on 30 October will be whether the process produces traceable case IDs and written outcomes, and whether people can escalate an inadequate answer without having to file the same facts again through another portal. Publishing aggregate numbers by scheme and state—received, resolved, pending, appealed, and reopened—would connect the digital intake to accountable field-level service.

### 4. TRINETRA makes public-space safety data-driven—and raises a safeguards question

At its 23 October National e-Governance Webinar, DARPG showcased TRINETRA, a system used around the Mahakal Rudrasagar Integrated Development Area and Mahakaleshwar Temple in Ujjain. The project presentation describes more than 450 AI-enabled cameras, video analytics, facial recognition, and predictive crowd monitoring through an Integrated Command & Control Centre. The project brief cited at the webinar reports that emergency response time fell from 15–20 minutes to under five minutes; it also lists 552 missing-person cases resolved, 53 lost items recovered, 10 theft incidents intercepted, and video evidence supplied in seven police cases.[^6]

These are project-reported outcomes, not an independent evaluation. Faster coordination during a crowded emergency can be valuable, but the release does not report how the facial-recognition component performs, whether it identifies people or only supports search after an incident, what data are retained, who can access footage, or how an incorrect match can be challenged. It also gives no comparison method for the response-time claim. These are not reasons to dismiss the system; they are the minimum questions needed to judge whether a safety tool is proportionate and accountable in a public place.

The webinar’s role was to share award-winning approaches for possible replication. Replication should therefore carry the safeguards and evaluation method with the cameras: publish retention and access rules, document human review before action, log and audit searches, and report error and complaint rates. A safety system that cannot explain how it handles a false identification is not yet a model ready to copy.

### 5. Panchayat Advancement Index turns local development into a common data framework

The same DARPG webinar presented the Ministry of Panchayati Raj’s Panchayat Advancement Index (PAI). The PIB release describes a development framework covering around 2.6 lakh Gram Panchayats, structured around nine Localized Sustainable Development Goal themes, 150 indicators, and 230 data points. It says the system combines standardised indicators, integrated data, validation workflows, dashboards, and Panchayat scorecards to help officials identify gaps and assign priorities. The Ministry presents it as a development report card rather than merely a ranking exercise.[^6]

Common measures can help a Gram Panchayat see where services are lagging and make planning more evidence-led. But a scorecard is only as good as its underlying records and the ability of local institutions to correct them. A national indicator set may make performance legible across places while obscuring differences in resources, population, geography, or reporting capacity. If data are missing or stale, a low score could reflect a service gap—or the weakness of the measurement system. The release does not detail who validates each data point, how often values are refreshed, or whether elected representatives and residents can challenge a disputed entry.

PAI’s value should be measured not by the number of Panchayats covered, but by whether a scorecard leads to funded action and a later, verifiable improvement. The public needs the indicator definitions, data sources, update dates, local correction route, and a way to track what action followed a flagged gap. That is the difference between a dashboard that describes government and one that helps residents hold it to account.

## Trends and implications

- **More public metrics, but not yet comparable outcomes.** Campaign targets show activity; consistent case definitions, backlog ages, and appeal outcomes would show whether redress improved.
- **Hybrid access is a governance feature.** DigiPost and the planned chaupals recognise that digital records need physical delivery and assisted, local resolution.
- **Data uses have different risk profiles.** Panchayat-level indicators support planning; camera-based facial recognition processes far more sensitive observations of people in public space. “Data-driven governance” is not one safeguards category.
- **Replication needs the accountability design, not only the software.** Human review, correction, appeal, retention limits, and independent evaluation should travel with the model.

## What to watch next

The 30 October WCD drive should publish its actual grievance totals and outcomes; Special Campaign 6 closes on 31 October, creating an opportunity for a final, department-comparable report; and DARPG’s showcased projects need public methods and safeguards before they are copied elsewhere. In the official releases reviewed for 17–24 October, these implementation signals—not a new CPGRAMS monthly scorecard—are the clearest L6 developments.
