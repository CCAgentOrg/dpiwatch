# DPI Brief — October 22, 2026

**Coverage window:** 21 October 2026, 08:00–22 October 2026, 08:00 IST.

The most consequential development was a CERT-In alert on compromised eMudhra public-web certificate keys. The window also brought NPCI’s daily transaction snapshot and the scheduled first day of a nationwide financial-inclusion camp drive. Most other DPI layers were quiet in the official feeds reviewed; absence of a new notice is not evidence that a service had no operational changes.

## 1. CERT-In warns of weak eMudhra intermediate CA keys (L7 — trust)

CERT-In’s [21 October advisory](https://www.cert-in.org.in/s2cMainServlet?CACODE=CICA-2026-3649&pageid=PUBADV01) says eight eMudhra intermediate certificate-authority keys were generated with insufficient randomness. The agency specifies that the affected P-384 elliptic-curve private-key values were smaller than 256 and could be recovered almost instantly from the corresponding public keys. That does not mean the P-384 algorithm itself is broken: the failure was in key generation. A recovered key could be used to create fraudulent TLS certificates, impersonate websites, or—if a client still trusts the chain and an attacker can intercept traffic—enable man-in-the-middle attacks. CERT-In also flags risk from compromised code-signing keys.

The agency reports eMudhra’s statement that none of the eight affected intermediates issued end-entity certificates, and that the affected intermediates—not the company’s root private keys—were in scope. CERT-In says the domestic Indian digital-signature-certificate and eSign infrastructure is unaffected, and that the public disclosures reviewed do not establish confirmed exploitation. That is a material distinction: this is a real trust-chain failure with potential for abuse, but the advisory does not report a confirmed victim or successful attack.

CERT-In asks administrators to update browsers and trust stores, identify certificates chaining to the affected intermediates or emSign ECC Root CA - G3, replace certificates that will no longer be trusted, and monitor Certificate Transparency logs. It also cautions against bypassing certificate warnings. The alert matters beyond TLS: public digital services rely on certificate trust to authenticate websites and signed software.

## 2. eMudhra narrows its impact account; root-store migration remains (L7 — trust; L3 — digital signatures)

In its [statement updated 21 October](https://emudhra.com/en/post/certificate-security-incident), eMudhra says the issue came from a flawed wrap/unwrap step in an April 2025 key-generation ceremony. It says seven affected intermediates were never used in production; one was deployed but issued no certificate. The company says the incident affects one customer ecosystem in the United States, that its Indian eSign and digital-signature services remain unaffected, and that it is replacing the customer’s certificates. These are the company’s own findings, not an independent audit conclusion; its investigation is still open.

Google’s [Chrome and Android response](https://blog.google/security/protecting-chrome-and-android-users-from-compromised-emudhra-intermediate-ca-certificates/) describes blocking the affected public-key hashes, with equivalent Android protection distributed through Google Play services. Google says the broader Chrome Root Store removal of emSign ECC Root CA - G3 is expected on 27 October; legitimate certificates under that root may stop working until replaced. Website operators should check their actual certificate chains and follow vendor guidance. CERT-In’s advisory also links to [Mozilla’s response](https://www.mozilla.org/en-US/security/advisories/mfsa2026-111/), including Firefox revocation-list and trust-store changes. These safeguards reduce exposure, but separate application, operating-system and embedded-device trust stores can lag.

## 3. NPCI publishes 20 October payment-rail figures (L2 — payments)

NPCI’s [daily statistics card posted on 21 October](https://x.com/NPCI_NPCI/status/2112863933891825884) records 787.89 million **BHIM UPI** transactions worth ₹75,358.48 crore on 20 October. It also reports 2.70 million AePS transactions worth ₹622.29 crore, 9.17 million IMPS transactions worth ₹14,342.97 crore, and 10.21 million NETC transactions worth ₹213.78 crore. The card is a one-day snapshot; BHIM UPI should not be read as a complete measure of all UPI activity, and volumes do not disclose success rates, reversals, fraud losses or bank-level outages.

## 4. Financial-inclusion drive reaches its scheduled start (L1, L2 and L6)

The Department of Financial Services’ [19 October notice](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2325230&lang=1) sets 21 October as the start of camps planned through 31 December across 2.70 lakh Gram Panchayats and Urban Local Bodies. Banks are to support PMJDY accounts, PMJJBY/PMSBY/APY enrolment, Aadhaar seeding for PMJJBY/PMSBY, re-KYC, pending Central KYC IDs, nomination updates, Kisan Credit Card applications and crop-insurance enrolment, alongside digital-fraud awareness. The start date falls inside this brief’s window, but the notice is a plan—not evidence that camps ran, applications succeeded or complaints were resolved. The useful next disclosure is district-level delivery and failure data, including whether people can complete services without unnecessary Aadhaar linking or repeat visits.

## Seven-layer scan

- **L1 Identity:** Camp drive includes Aadhaar seeding and re-KYC; no new UIDAI rule located.
- **L2 Payments:** NPCI’s daily rail figures above; no new UPI rule identified.
- **L3 Documents:** No new DigiLocker, API Setu or eSign platform notice; CERT-In says Indian eSign is not affected.
- **L4 Commerce:** No qualifying new ONDC or GeM update found.
- **L5 Sectoral:** No qualifying new ABHA/ABDM or AgriStack update found.
- **L6 Governance:** The DFS camp plan is the relevant local-delivery item; no new CPGRAMS or eOffice change found.
- **L7 Trust:** CERT-In’s eMudhra advisory is the major update; no separate new DPDP or TRAI item located.

**Watch next:** Confirmation of camp delivery and exclusions; replacement of certificates under the distrusted root; and public reporting on failed or reversed payment transactions, not only throughput.
