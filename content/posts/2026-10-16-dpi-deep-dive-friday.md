# DPI Deep Dive — Friday | October 16, 2026

**Focus layer:** L5 — Sectoral Infrastructure: agriculture, health, and public-service systems built for specific domains.

**Coverage period:** 9–16 October 2026 (IST). The window’s most concrete signals are a year-one dashboard account for agricultural districts, an official push to digitise Forest Rights Act records, a global governance forum on AI in food systems, and new Ayurveda digital tools. A national e-governance conference scheduled for 16–17 October puts several of these systems on one programme—but, at this publication cutoff, its agenda is evidence of what will be presented, not of new launches or outcomes.

## Executive summary

This week’s L5 story is less “another app launched” than the harder work of connecting a sector’s records, decisions, and services. PM Dhan-Dhaanya Krishi Yojana (PMDDKY) reports a dashboard and 121 indicators across 100 agricultural districts. The Ministry of Tribal Affairs (MoTA) demonstrated a national Forest Rights Act (FRA) portal and mobile app, while asking states to digitise records, reconcile discrepancies, and link systems. A UN-coordinated initiative discussed governance for agricultural AI; the All India Institute of Ayurveda (AIIA) launched two clinical apps alongside a startup portal and innovation facility.

These are different kinds of infrastructure at different levels of maturity. Programme dashboards and portals can make administration more legible, but they do not by themselves prove better outcomes, valid records, trustworthy recommendations, or accessible care. The central test is whether data systems help people exercise rights and receive services—and whether they can correct errors, challenge decisions, and obtain human help when the technology fails.

## Key developments

### 1. PMDDKY’s first-year dashboard turns district plans into a measurable programme

A PIB backgrounder posted on 10 October marked the first anniversary of PM Dhan-Dhaanya Krishi Yojana. The programme covers 100 “Aspirational Agricultural Districts,” chosen using low productivity, low cropping intensity, and low agricultural credit disbursement, with additional consideration of cropped area and operational holdings. The government says it converges 36 Central schemes across 11 ministries and departments, together with state programmes and private participation. District Action Plans are monitored through 121 indicators—74 output and 47 outcome measures—and monthly output rankings appear on the PM-DDKY dashboard.[^1]

The official account says every selected district had prepared a plan and submitted output data through August 2026, while all 100 had submitted outcome data for FY 2025–26. It reports an average score rising from 18.85 in April to 46.89 in August. That is a substantial increase in the programme’s composite score, but the score is not itself evidence that farmers’ incomes rose by a corresponding amount or that the dashboard caused improvements. Nor should a monthly ranking be mistaken for an independently audited result. The backgrounder does not, in the account summarised here, provide enough detail to interpret the score as a direct measure of household welfare.

Useful next disclosures would include the denominator and calculation for each indicator, corrections to submitted data, reasons for missing values, and evidence connecting funded interventions to outcomes on farms.

### 2. Forest-rights digitisation is being tied to records, maps, and post-claim support

At a regional review conference in Kolkata on 9 October, MoTA asked eight states to address pending Forest Rights Act claims, digitise case records, incorporate recognised rights into revenue and forest records, reconcile data, and prepare action plans. Officials demonstrated a national FRA portal and mobile app, and urged states to integrate existing records through APIs. The ministry’s release cited, as of 31 August, more than 54 lakh claims received nationally, about 25 lakh titles distributed, around 11 lakh claims pending, and more than 18 lakh rejected.[^2]

This is a particularly consequential form of sectoral digital infrastructure because the record is not just a service ticket: it can affect whether a community’s legally recognised land and forest rights are visible in the administrative systems that govern access to schemes, credit, and livelihoods. Digitisation may reduce fragmented files and help track a case across departments. Mapping and incorporation into official records may also make a recognised title usable after the claim stage. But an API connection is not proof that the underlying record is correct, current, or legally sufficient.

A system must distinguish a pending claim from a rejected one, preserve the grounds and review history, and provide a route to correct mismatched names, boundaries, or status. If rejected claims are digitised without a visible review path, the portal could make an old administrative decision look final simply because it is neatly recorded. Conversely, if a recognised right is not entered into relevant land records, the claimant may hold a title that downstream agencies cannot see.

The release also stresses Gram Sabha capacity and authority, including community forest resource plans. Digital workflows should therefore assist—not displace—the Gram Sabha’s statutory role or the participation of rights-holders. The next meaningful evidence is not a count of portal logins or API links; it is whether cases are disposed of lawfully, recognised rights are reflected accurately, and people can inspect and challenge their records without needing a specialist intermediary.

### 3. The AI-for-agriculture conversation is moving toward governance, not a national service launch

On 10 October, the Department of Telecommunications’ Telecommunication Engineering Centre and ICAR hosted the second meeting of the UN-coordinated Global Initiative on AI for Agri-Food Systems (GI-AI4FS), alongside India Mobile Congress. A two-day hackathon had taken place on 8–9 October. PIB says the meeting brought together about 70 in-person delegates and 25 online participants; it considered a proposed governance and coordination structure, working-group roles and terms of reference, and initial workstreams. The technologies discussed included connected sensors, weather stations, drones, satellites, and AI analytics for tasks such as crop-disease detection, resource use, traceability, and supply-chain efficiency.[^3]

The difference between “a potential use” and a dependable farmer-facing service is the point. The release documents a meeting, showcased hackathon prototypes, and governance deliberation. It does not announce that a particular AI tool has been deployed nationally, validated across crops and regions, or adopted by farmers. That distinction matters when AI is framed as helping farmers make decisions: a recommendation about disease or inputs can affect a season’s income, not just a screen’s convenience.

For L5, the question is whether common standards and public infrastructure can make useful innovations interoperable without making farmers’ data a raw material that they cannot govern. A credible programme needs to say what data a model uses, how it performs across local conditions and languages, who tests it, and who is accountable when a prediction is wrong. It should also explain whether a farmer can decline participation without losing access to unrelated schemes, and how a person can reach an agricultural extension worker when a model’s answer is unclear. 

### 4. AIIA’s new Ayurveda tools raise a practical question: what exactly is being digitised?

On 15 October, the Vice-President inaugurated KAUMARACON-2026 at AIIA, where the institute launched the Ayush Start-up Portal, the AIIA–iCAINE website, the Ayurveda Digital Innovation and Venture Acceleration Facility (AyuDIVA), the Vamana App, and the Panchakarma Assessment App. PIB presents these as digital infrastructure, entrepreneurship, and technology-enabled clinical practice initiatives. The conference is focused on Ayurvedic paediatrics and runs from 15 to 17 October.[^4]

A named app is a launch milestone, not a service-quality result. The release does not specify the apps’ exact functions, intended users, data collected, storage arrangements, interoperability with ABDM or ABHA, privacy terms, clinical validation, or uptake. Those omissions do not establish that the tools are unsafe; they define what cannot yet be responsibly claimed from the announcement alone. In health, patient records and assessments can be sensitive, and a clinical tool’s interface can influence decisions even when the software is described as an aid.

The portal and innovation facility may help connect researchers, practitioners, and startups, while the two apps could support clinical workflows. To assess their public value, publish product documentation: intended use and limits, privacy notice, data retention and sharing, accessibility, version history, and a channel for reporting errors. If the apps produce or store patient information, explain how a patient can access, correct, or revoke sharing where applicable. If an assessment influences care, make clear who remains clinically responsible. Linking to ABDM should be stated only if that integration exists and its consent flow is documented; the launch itself does not establish it.

### 5. Dharamshala’s conference puts sectoral systems on the agenda—not under evaluation

A 15 October DARPG release said a National Conference on e-Governance Reforms would take place in Dharamshala on 16–17 October. Its announced programme includes AgriStack, e-Jagriti, an AI-enabled Clinical Decision Support System in eSanjeevani, a multi-hazard early-warning decision-support system, and iGOT Karmayogi. The release also lists presentations on healthcare, judicial administration, and citizen-facing services, with day two focused on cybersecurity, analytics, and replication of initiatives.[^5]

This is a useful cross-layer signal: sectoral infrastructure is being presented alongside governance, cybersecurity, and service-delivery practices. AgriStack’s data and identity rails, eSanjeevani’s clinical workflows, and digital judicial administration each depend on reliable records, access controls, and correction processes. But the release is a conference notice. At this publication cutoff, it offers no conference findings, independent assessment, adoption metrics, or technical detail about the listed systems. It does not support a claim that eSanjeevani’s clinical decision support has been newly deployed, or that a particular eCourts capability has changed this week.

The right follow-up is to ask for the presentations and evidence behind them. For each system, disclose its user group, rollout coverage, service completion and failure rates, complaint route, and security and data-governance arrangements. “Replicable” should mean that the conditions for success and failure are documented—not simply that a slide deck can be copied from one state to another.

## Trends and implications

Across agriculture, forest rights, and health, this week’s releases show a growing emphasis on data linkage and measurement: district dashboards, digitised case files, APIs, AI tools, and clinical apps. The systems are not interchangeable. A district performance dashboard is not a farmer registry; an FRA record is a rights-bearing administrative file; a clinical assessment tool handles health-related decisions. Each needs its own rules for accuracy, access, correction, retention, and appeal.

The cross-layer connection is clear: identity, documents, connectivity, sectoral registers, and grievance channels must work together, but integration magnifies both utility and error. A mistaken land record may propagate across departments; a flawed model may repeat across districts; a health app may create sensitive records without a clear path to correction. The value of DPI should therefore be judged by completed services and rights exercised—not merely account counts, data layers, apps released, or dashboards switched on.

## What to watch next

- PMDDKY: indicator definitions, district-level data, and independent evidence of outcomes beyond the composite score.
- FRA: state-level digitisation timelines, record-correction and review workflows, and incorporation of recognised titles into revenue and forest records.
- GI-AI4FS: published working-group terms, farmer representation, model testing standards, and accountability for harmful recommendations.
- AIIA: user documentation, privacy and clinical-governance details, and any verified ABDM/ABHA integration.
- Dharamshala: conference proceedings and system-level evidence, especially for eSanjeevani’s AI support and judicial-administration presentations.
