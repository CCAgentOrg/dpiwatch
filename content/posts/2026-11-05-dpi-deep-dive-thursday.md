# DPI Deep Dive — Thursday | November 05, 2026

## Executive summary

This week the commerce layer ran on two clocks. In Delhi, DPIIT and ONDC convened 40-plus FMCG leaders at Vanijya Bhawan under the Bharat Commerce Chintan Shivir and disclosed that DigiDukaan — the network's kirana-as-buyer procurement rail — had nearly doubled its retailer base in a quarter, from about 12,000 to over 21,000 across Hyderabad, Jaipur and Mumbai, while the wider ONDC network crossed 500 million cumulative transactions.[^1][^2] In Mumbai, CDSL — the depository behind 19.34 crore demat accounts — disclosed a strategic investment in ONDC alongside its Q2 results, putting market-infrastructure-class money behind the DPIIT-backed network for the first time.[^3][^4] And in Pune, a startup delivering through ONDC and direct APIs reported an 18-fold monthly order jump since June and raised a ₹5 crore pre-seed on those numbers.[^5][^6] Under it all sat GeM's seller-economics reset announced at GeM Samvad on October 28 — transaction charges cut from 0.50% to 0.30%, the zero-charge threshold doubled to ₹10 lakh — whose working-capital logic is the same one DigiDukaan is chasing in kirana procurement.[^7][^8] The layer's question has moved from "does open commerce work?" to "who funds it, who bears the cost, and can adoption scale beyond pilots?"

### 1. DigiDukaan doubles to 21,000 kiranas — but the roundtable is really about identity, credit and logistics

The week's anchor event was the October 30 CEO roundtable chaired by Commerce and Industry Minister Piyush Goyal, convened by DPIIT and ONDC at Vanijya Bhawan.[^1] The headline number: DigiDukaan had connected over 21,000 retailers with more than 450 brands across Hyderabad, Jaipur and Mumbai as of September 2026, up from over 12,000 the previous quarter.[^1] Hyderabad leads with 16,000-plus onboarded retailers and monthly repeat-order rates of 73–78%, and an initial ONDC retailer study found 40% of surveyed retailers reporting lower inventory holdings for DigiDukaan-procured products — early evidence the B2B rail moves working capital, not just orders.[^1][^3]

The government's own framing of the release is more revealing than the headline. DigiDukaan digitises kiranas as *buyers*: one interface to discover and order across multiple brands, distributors and wholesalers, with visibility into pricing, trade schemes and availability — explicitly designed to strengthen existing distribution relationships rather than replace them.[^1] ONDC MD and CEO Vibhor Jain put the baseline bluntly: distributor-side workflows are largely online, but retailers "still run on pen, paper and memory."[^1] The roundtable's five deliberation areas read like a shopping list for a full-stack commerce DPI: a common digital identity for kirana retailers to engage brands, distributors and service providers; shared logistics and micro-warehousing to enable next-day replenishment even beyond existing delivery routes; formal credit linked to digital procurement records to reduce dependence on informal distributor credit; plug-and-play Distributor Management System connectors and standardised catalogues; and realigned commercial incentives so digital ordering pays for FMCG companies, sales teams, distributors and channel partners alike.[^1]

The ecosystem around the rail is now visible in the release itself: HUL, Marico, Colgate-Palmolive, Emami and Amul participate through their distribution networks, while ITC, Tata Consumer Products and Britannia are at various stages of integration or discussion; Paytm for Business, Qwipo and Mulltiply.ai are live as technology providers, with Bizom, Botree, QuIDPe and ENS integrating on the DMS side; and ElasticRun, Shiprocket, India Post and Moeving are plugged in for logistics and warehousing.[^1] The National Traders' Welfare Board, chaired by Sunil J. Singhi, is mobilising local trade associations for adoption, and the next phase targets deeper brand and distributor participation in North Mumbai.[^1]

The honest denominator: India has roughly 1.4 crore kirana stores, so 21,000 onboarded retailers is about 0.15% of the universe.[^12] What makes this week different from previous DigiDukaan updates is the direction of the pitch — from store-facing digitisation to the financial plumbing underneath (identity, transaction-linked credit, shared logistics). If the credit piece lands, kirana procurement records become underwriting data; if it lands through consented rails, it is a consumer win, because 73–78% repeat ordering means the data trail is a durable picture of a shop's economics. The risk is the quiet one: informal distributor credit being replaced not by formal, consented credit but by platform-scored credit with terms the shop owner never read.

### 2. CDSL takes a strategic stake in ONDC: a depository buys into the commerce layer

On November 1, CDSL reported Q2 results — consolidated net profit up 24% year-on-year to ₹173.9 crore, revenue up 23.7% to ₹395 crore, around 75 lakh new demat accounts added in the quarter taking the base to 19.34 crore, and assets under custody crossing ₹89 lakh crore — and in the same disclosure revealed a strategic investment in the Open Network for Digital Commerce.[^3][^4] Shares rose about 3% on Monday, November 2.[^3]

A market infrastructure institution buying into a DPIIT-backed Section 8 commerce network matters twice over. First, it extends a pattern worth tracking: established DPI operators bankrolling newer network layers rather than waiting for them to mature — the same logic that has pulled banks and NBFCs into ONDC's credit and financial-services protocols. CDSL's core product is verified ownership records; ONDC's DigiDukaan proposition is verified transaction records for credit underwriting. The thematic adjacency is not accidental, and it is exactly the kind of cross-infrastructure capital the network model needs now that grant-era funding has faded. Second, it raises the question the disclosure leaves unanswered: what does the stake buy? Network participation, settlement infrastructure for marketplace flows, or plain balance-sheet support? ONDC does not publish its cap table's strategic logic, and CDSL's release does not say.[^3][^4]

For the consumer, the interesting angle is trust portability. The depository whose job is making ownership records trustworthy is now an owner of the network whose job is making commerce records interoperable. If that leads to settlement-grade guarantees on ONDC transactions — dispute resolution, escrow-like protections, verified counterparty records — it addresses the layer's weakest consumer surface: what happens when an open-network order goes wrong, with no single platform to complain to. Watch whether CDSL's quarterly disclosures start describing operational integration rather than just the investment line.

### 3. Dispatch Network: 18x in five months — the interoperable-logistics thesis gets funded

On November 2, Pune-based Dispatch Network raised a ₹5 crore pre-seed led by Antler India, on the strength of growth numbers from a network that runs through direct APIs and ONDC alongside five major platforms — Zomato, Swiggy, Zepto, Instamart and BigBasket.[^5][^6] Since launching in June 2026, the company reports monthly deliveries growing from 3,014 orders in June to 55,000 in October — roughly 18x — now processing around 3,500 deliveries a day.[^5]

The claimed unit economics are the real story: empty kilometres cut by two-thirds, riders earning about 55% more per month than the industry average, and rider retention around 80% higher — though the company did not publish its baseline, so these are founder figures, not audited ones.[^7] Still, the shape of the claim fits the open-logistics thesis: if a rider can receive orders from multiple demand sources on one network, utilisation rises, and higher utilisation is the only durable way to raise rider pay without raising takeaway fees. ONDC's own logistics layer gives the context — more than 50 logistics service providers, over 60,000 merchants, 150,000 delivery partners, and upwards of 12,000 interoperable deliveries a day — numbers Dispatch sits alongside rather than inside.[^9][^10]

Two caveats keep this a watch-story rather than a verdict. First, 55,000 monthly orders is a pilot-scale wedge in a market where single platforms process multiples of that daily; the Mumbai launch planned for November and the stated target of 50,000 orders a day across five cities are the tests.[^6][^8] Second, "works with ONDC" has been a marketing line for many startups; the durable question is what share of Dispatch's volume actually flows through interoperable protocols versus proprietary platform contracts. If the open share grows as the platform share matures, this is evidence the network model can seed businesses that platforms alone would not have hosted — and it is precisely the kind of company ONDC's backers said the network would create.

### 4. GeM's seller-economics reset: the B2G rail quietly repriced while capital chased the B2C one

The commerce layer's other rail — Government e-Marketplace, the public-procurement platform — made its move one day before this window opens, at GeM Samvad 2026 with Laghu Udyog Bharati on October 28, and its implications ran through the week's coverage. GeM cut transaction charges from 0.50% to 0.30% of order value, raised the charge-exemption threshold from ₹5 lakh to ₹10 lakh, and capped charges for orders above ₹10 crore at ₹3 lakh — down from a theoretical maximum of ₹72.5 lakh.[^7][^8] Nearly 97% of orders already attract no transaction charge, and OEM vendor-assessment fees fell by up to 92%.[^8] GeM has also integrated with all five TReDS exchanges — per the Union Budget 2026–27 announcement — alongside GeM SAHAY's collateral-free lending against purchase orders, so the working-capital side moved as much as the fee schedule.[^8]

Scale context, from GeM's own disclosures this year: the platform has crossed ₹18.4 lakh crore in cumulative GMV, including more than ₹5 lakh crore in procurement during FY 2025–26; MSEs took 68% of order count and 47.1% of GMV that year, with over 11 lakh MSEs registered securing 51 lakh-plus orders worth ₹2.36 lakh crore — up over 20% — women-led MSEs crossed ₹28,000 crore (up nearly 28%), startups ₹19,000 crore (up 36%), and state procurement grew 38.3%.[^11]

Put the two rails side by side and the week's economics rhyme. GeM cut the cost of selling to government and wired invoice-financing rails; ONDC's DigiDukaan is building the buyer-side rail for kiranas with transaction-linked formal credit on the agenda; CDSL's capital is chasing the network's trust layer; and Antler's cheque is funding a logistics mesh whose pitch is higher rider utilisation. The common thread is working capital as the layer's real product — procurement records, order flows and delivery histories becoming the collateral of small sellers who never had formal credit. The consumer stake is straightforward: when seller costs fall on open rails, some of it should reach prices; the open question, as ever with open networks, is whether competition on the network or network fees capture the saving.

## Cross-layer connections

Three threads tie the commerce week to the rest of the stack. First, L4 to L1 (identity): the roundtable's first deliberation area — a common digital identity for kirana retailers — lands in the same fortnight the identity layer matured its verifiable-credential posture, from Aadhaar App credentials accepted as government ID in Nagaland to Digi Yatra's e-passport credentials. A retailer digital ID built on that architecture would let one shop engage every brand, distributor and lender without re-onboarding at each counter — or become another siloed login if the network cuts corners. Second, L4 to L2 (payments and credit): DigiDukaan's procurement-linked credit, GeM SAHAY and TReDS integration are all bets that transaction records can underwrite small businesses; those records ride on UPI and bank rails, and their consent architecture is DPDP territory — L7's jurisdiction. A kirana's order history is now a credit file; who may read it, and on what consent, is the privacy question hiding inside the commerce story. Third, L4 to L6 (governance): GeM's AI-driven catalogue validation, bid health scores and anomaly detection are governance infrastructure inside a commerce platform — procurement integrity as code. The same analytics that catch collusion among sellers can, in the wrong configuration, quietly delist a seller with no human in the loop; the layer needs its grievance channels to grow as fast as its pricing engines.

**What to watch next week:** whether CDSL's next disclosure describes *what* the ONDC stake buys — participation, settlement, or balance sheet; DigiDukaan's next quarterly number and the North Mumbai deepening, plus any published FMCG commitments on incentive realignment; Dispatch Network's Mumbai launch and progress toward 50,000 orders a day; GeM's order-flow and TReDS-volume response to the fee cuts; and whether the kirana digital-identity work streams into India's verifiable-credential architecture or forks from it.
