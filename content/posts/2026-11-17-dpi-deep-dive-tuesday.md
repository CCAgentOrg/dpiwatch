# DPI Deep Dive — Tuesday | November 17, 2026

**Focus Layer:** L2 — Payments & Financial Rails: NPCI, UPI, RuPay  
**Coverage Period:** November 10 – November 17, 2026

## Executive summary

The payments layer spent the week holding a fee whose launch date has now come and gone twice. On November 11 — two days after Deepavali — NPCI confirmed to The Hindu that there was still "no announcement" on when the 0.4% merchant discount rate on select UPI transactions would go live, despite its October 13 commitment to implement it "immediately post Diwali."[^1] The same day, NPCI circulated the UPI Vyapaar Saksham Scheme, a three-year small-merchant acceptance programme funded by 5% of members' MDR collections — subsidy infrastructure being built for a revenue stream that does not yet legally exist.[^2] The Supreme Court record, meanwhile, now holds both operators' core commitments in writing: NPCI's November 3 affidavit (the levy touches only ~4% of P2M transactions; pass-through is a binding prohibition, not a promise) and the Centre's November 4 affidavit (nobody was ever assured zero MDR forever; the subsidy was discretionary).[^3][^4] October's data closed the loop: a record 25.47 billion transactions worth ₹31.53 lakh crore, with average daily value crossing ₹1 lakh crore for the first time — and a frequency-value paradox (63% of volume but 31% of value sits in merchant payments) that explains both why the government is taxing this layer and why the tax is engineered to stay small.[^5][^6] And RuPay credit cards on UPI — the one UPI flow explicitly exempt from the new MDR — revealed a two-tier app economy: the two biggest apps own 77% of UPI volume but trail CRED, Paytm and HDFC Bank on every credit feature that matters.[^7]

## Key developments

### 1. The MDR date that wouldn't be announced

The week's defining non-event was the absent notification. The sequence: NPCI's UPI and Services Steering Committee decided on October 13 that, "keeping in mind the festive season," MDR on select P2M transactions would be implemented "immediately post Diwali at a date to be notified separately" — after the original October 15 go-live was shelved. The Hindu reported at the time that the delay was decided in the Prime Minister's Office following strong appeals from traders, with CAIT's Praveen Khandelwal publicly welcoming the deferment as protection for traders' festive-season momentum.[^1] Diwali fell on November 8, Bhai Dooj on November 11 — and by November 10, merchant-facing coverage was openly asking for clarity, noting that merchants and customers were waiting while petrol-pump dealers reiterated that MDR, stacked on a dealer commission unrevised for years, would squeeze margins again.[^8] On November 11, an NPCI spokesperson told The Hindu there was "no announcement on this at the moment."[^1] Through November 12–13 the silence held: investor coverage framed the delay as a pending recurring-revenue unlock for Paytm, MobiKwik and Pine Labs, while explainer pieces confirmed that as of November 13 no replacement effective date had been confirmed anywhere.[^9][^10] One unconfirmed industry report said November 15 had been discussed internally, contingent on a post-Diwali stakeholder meeting that had not yet happened.[^11]

The analytical point is that limbo is not neutral. A merchant cannot reprice, an acquirer cannot build billing logic, and a PSP cannot test settlement changes against a date that does not exist. Each deferred day converts what should be a planned cutover into a surprise — and the eventual go-live inherits all the festive-season goodwill spending the deferral was meant to protect. The deeper governance observation sits in the PMO detail: a fee calendar for a systemically important network utility is now effectively sequenced by trader politics, and the operator's own steering-committee process is downstream of that veto. Whatever date finally lands, the precedent — that notified payment-system economics can be re-sequenced by appeal to the political centre between announcement and implementation — is now part of the layer's operating model.

### 2. Vyapaar Saksham: spending the MDR before it exists

While the MDR date stayed dark, the spending plan went live. In a circular dated November 11 (OC No. 242/2026-27, to all UPI members), NPCI introduced the UPI Vyapaar Saksham Scheme to expand acceptance infrastructure among small merchants — funded through contributions from the MDR collections of acquiring banks, issuer banks and PSPs, and scheduled to run for three years from the date UPI MDR is implemented.[^2] The scheme targets small merchants in Tier 3–6 centres, the North-East, Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh, with reported turnover eligibility up to ₹40 lakh; the incentive pool is split 30/50/20 across new QR and soundbox onboarding, growth-linked incentives, and incremental volumes in the target geographies.[^12] The per-unit payouts — ₹300 for every physical QR and ₹750 for every soundbox deployed at an eligible new small merchant — go to the acquiring banks and payment providers, not to shopkeepers directly, alongside transaction-linked incentives for sustained usage.[^13] The circular's exclusion list is itself a document: capital-market transactions (mutual funds, securities, brokers and dealers) are carved out of the incentive-eligible flow from day one, and the funding mechanism hooks back to the September 15 MDR framework circular (OC No. 237/2026-27) that set the levy up in the first place.[^14]

Two things deserve scrutiny. First, the design quietly concedes the economics of acceptance: a ₹750 subsidy to make a soundbox deployment viable is an admission that payment-notification hardware at small-ticket densities is a bank-margin business, not a merchant-choice one — the same lesson the card industry learned two decades ago. Second, the cross-subsidy NPCI has constructed deserves to be argued in public, not just operationalised in circulars: a scheme funded by a levy collected overwhelmingly from larger-ticket commerce will hand hardware subsidies to merchants in Tier 3–6 towns — a defensible redistribution, but one whose beneficiaries and payers should be able to read the math. As of this writing, coverage of the scheme relied on the member circular and press reviews; it was not yet listed on NPCI's public UPI circulars page — a transparency gap for a programme funded by a mandatory contribution on member revenue.[^12]

### 3. The courtroom ledger: what has actually been promised

The litigation backdrop hardened this window, and it is now the only place the system's commitments exist in writing. The PIL by advocate Anjan Datta challenges the September 14 notification and the revised MDR framework; the Supreme Court declined a stay on September 28 and sought responses.[^15] NPCI's counter-affidavit, filed November 3 and reported through November 5, argues that zero MDR is "no longer sustainable" because functional costs of a phenomenally expanding system would otherwise burn into the public exchequer; that the levy affects only about 4% of P2M transactions above ₹2,000, with P2M payments up to ₹2,000 "entirely free"; and that the pass-through prohibition is binding — a payer bank or UPI app that lets merchants surcharge would breach its SLA obligations and face compliance action or termination of membership.[^3][^16] The Centre's affidavit, filed November 4, completes the picture: no specific assurance of a continuing zero-MDR regime was ever given to merchants, and the subsidy the State provided was discretionary rather than obligatory.[^4]

The economic claim inside these affidavits deserves its own test. "Merchants cannot pass it on" is an SLA-enforcement theory, not an economics theory — and the evidence for informal pass-through predates the levy entirely. An ISB survey published November 4 found suppliers already charging vendors extra for accepting UPI payments before any MDR exists — documented, present-tense pass-through in the business-to-business direction.[^17] That is precisely the gap the All India Consumer Products Distributors Federation targeted in its November 16 letter to Finance Minister Sitharaman: exclude merchant-to-merchant (M2M) transfers from the MDR framework, examine the GST implications of MDR charges — a tax treatment layered onto a fee that itself attracts GST compounds the cost through the distribution chain — and hold proper stakeholder consultations before implementation.[^18] The FMCG distribution channel is exactly where M2M UPI runs heaviest: distributors paying for stock, not shopping. If the framework's final shape ignores that flow, its first visible effect will be a repricing negotiation between brands and distributors that the customer eventually underwrites.

### 4. October's record — and the frequency-value paradox

The month the deferral was announced, the system set its record. October delivered 25.47 billion transactions worth ₹31.53 lakh crore — volume up 5.8% and value up 7.4% over September, 23% and 16% respectively year-on-year — with daily averages of 821.5 million transactions and ₹1,01,707 crore in value, the first month above ₹1 lakh crore per day; 758 banks were live on the network, up from 756.[^5][^6] The Diwali spike was real and measurable: on festival day itself, UPI volume jumped 32% on last-minute buying.[^19] The P2P/P2M split published during this window shows the structural shape underneath: merchant payments were 16.09 billion transactions — 63.2% of volume — but carried only ₹9.71 lakh crore, 30.8% of value; person-to-person transfers were 9.37 billion transactions carrying ₹21.82 lakh crore.[^6][^13] Computed from that split, the average merchant ticket was roughly ₹603 against roughly ₹2,328 for a person-to-person transfer — nearly four times larger.

That is the frequency-value paradox, and it is the MDR design's arithmetic in one picture. P2M is where the frequency lives (and the political sensitivity with it); P2P is where the value lives (and where the tax never lands). A 0.4% levy capped at ₹300, confined to the ~4% of P2M transactions above ₹2,000, taxes a thin, high-value slice of the merchant flow while leaving the mass habit untouched — by NPCI's own court filing, 96% of merchant payments are meant to stay unaffected.[^3][^9] The same data carries a quieter warning: value growth (16%) is trailing volume growth (23%) year-on-year, meaning the average transaction is shrinking — the small-ticket habit is deepening, which is good for adoption and structurally bad for any revenue model hoping to harvest meaningful collections from above-threshold tickets alone. Both the Vyapaar Saksham fund and the sustainability argument NPCI filed in court assume collections worth redistributing; October's mix says the base being taxed is narrower than the headline suggests.

### 5. RuPay credit cards on UPI: the two-tier app economy

The most revealing dataset of the week arrived November 16: NPCI's own numbers show the two dominant UPI apps — PhonePe and Google Pay, together 77.4% of all UPI transactions in September — trailing far smaller rivals on every RuPay credit-card feature on UPI.[^7] In October's data: Paytm alone held 40% of RuPay credit card AutoPay transactions on UPI against 22.8% for PhonePe and Google Pay combined; CRED led biometric authentication with 54.7% of its RuPay credit card transactions up to ₹10,000 approved by fingerprint or face scan (Kiwi 29.14%, Scapia 26.28%); and HDFC Bank's app led EMI conversion at 19.6% of such spending by value, ahead of PhonePe at 17.7%.[^7] Industry sources told Business Standard the large players are simply not competing on this flow.

The context matters. RuPay credit cards are the only credit cards permitted on UPI — an exclusivity NPCI owns on both sides, card network and rail. And this is the flow the new MDR framework explicitly does not touch: the framework applies to direct account-to-merchant account-to-account UPI, with UPI-linked credit card payments excluded — meaning the interchange-earning flow stays outside the levy while bank-account flows above ₹2,000 take it.[^9] The feature matrix shows what the incentive structure produces: credit-on-UPI adoption is being pulled by niche fintechs monetising credit features (EMIs, rewards, biometric convenience), not by the mass-market apps whose business case for pushing credit lines into ₹603-average-ticket grocery payments is weak. Two implications to watch. If banks plough MDR proceeds into soundboxes, tech and merchant expansion as they have signalled, the acceptance infrastructure credit-on-UPI rides on gets built on the back of a levy that excludes credit transactions — a cross-subsidy from taxed bank-account flows to untaxed card flows.[^20] And the biometrics curve — led at 54.7% by an app with a fraction of PhonePe's base — shows PIN-replacement adoption is real but being slowed by big-app inertia, which is the opposite of how a default should diffuse.

## Cross-layer watch

- **L7 — Security, Privacy & Trust:** RBI issued its consolidated Cyber Resilience and Digital Payment Security Controls Master Directions for non-bank PSOs on November 5, replacing the July 2024 directions and consolidating scattered circulars into a single compliance surface for wallet and gateway operators; analysis of it landed in this window.[^21] The security bill the MDR's sustainability argument points to is being raised by regulation in the same fortnight its funding mechanism is being litigated — the layer's cost story and its security story are now the same story.
- **L6 — Governance:** the PMO-reported sequencing of the MDR date is a governance datum about who steers DPI: a network utility's fee calendar moved by trader politics between announcement and implementation.[^1]
- **L4 — Commerce:** the merchant-payment frequency being taxed is partly the transit and marketplace habit NPCI spent the year cultivating — last week's ONDC metro-ticketing volumes ride these same rails, meaning the MDR's boundary lines will decide whether a ₹20 metro ticket and a ₹2,000 retail cart ever share a fee schedule.
