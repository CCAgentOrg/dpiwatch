# DPI Deep Dive — Saturday | October 17, 2026

**Focus layer:** L6 — Governance & Grievance: CPGRAMS, DARPG, digital administration, and the systems through which citizens seek an answer from the state.

**Coverage period:** 10–17 October 2026 (IST). The week’s clearest L6 signals are the release of two September CPGRAMS scorecards, a mid-month account of digital file and grievance clearances under Special Campaign 6, and the National Conference on e-Governance Reforms in Dharamshala. A key distinction runs through all three: published activity and disposal counts are evidence of administrative throughput—not, by themselves, proof that a citizen received a complete, correct, or satisfactory remedy.

## Executive summary

DARPG published September grievance data for both Central Ministries and States/UTs on 13 October. Central departments reported 2,38,579 disposals against 2,27,837 new cases, with 85,687 pending; States/UTs reported 1,15,168 disposals against 99,134 new cases, with 2,37,128 pending. These are separate reporting streams and should not be collapsed into a single national performance score. The state backlog declined about 6.3% month-on-month, yet 23 States/UTs still had more than 1,000 pending cases apiece.

The reports also describe Common Service Centre (CSC) access, user feedback, and the integration of West Bengal’s portal. A separate Department of Food and Public Distribution (DFPD) update records 7,040 e-files closed by 15 October under Special Campaign 6, alongside thousands of physical-file reviews and progress against public-grievance targets. Meanwhile, the Dharamshala conference placed grievance systems, sectoral platforms, cybersecurity, data analytics, and replication on a shared agenda. It is a forum for presentations and exchange; the available releases do not yet provide an independent evaluation of the systems discussed.

## 1. CPGRAMS’ two scorecards show throughput—and the backlog that throughput has not erased

DARPG’s 53rd monthly report for Central Ministries/Departments and 50th report for States/UTs were both posted on 13 October, covering September 2026. The central report records 2,27,837 new public grievances, 2,38,579 disposed, and 85,687 pending. Average disposal time across Central Ministries/Departments in 2026 was 13 days, and monthly disposal exceeded 100,000 cases for the 51st consecutive month. On the state side, 99,134 cases were received and 1,15,168 disposed; the reported backlog stood at 2,37,128, down approximately 6.3% from August. Twenty-three States/UTs nevertheless had more than 1,000 pending grievances.[^1][^2]

The gap between flows and stock matters. Disposals exceeding new receipts can shrink a backlog, but cannot tell us whether old cases are being resolved equitably, whether a disposal answers the actual complaint, or whether the citizen had to appeal. The central report lists 48,653 appeals received and 42,116 disposed in September, with 23,230 appeals pending. That is an important second-stage measure: a first-level closure is not necessarily the end of a dispute.

The state figure also requires context. Uttar Pradesh reported 45,970 disposals in September, far ahead of Andhra Pradesh at 8,340 and Haryana at 6,437. A large absolute count can reflect population, inflow, staffing, or a major campaign; it is not a clean comparison of each resident’s chance of receiving a remedy. The release does not give a per-capita or complaint-category-adjusted ranking in its summary. Nor should a 6.3% decline in state backlog be read as a uniform improvement: the same report says 23 jurisdictions still had more than 1,000 pending grievances.

These reports are useful because they expose a public-service system’s workload and make comparisons possible. But the headline metric is still administrative disposal. To judge substantive redress, the public needs consistent definitions of “disposed,” breakdowns by complaint type and age, appeal outcomes, the share of complainants who report that the issue was actually resolved, and an audit trail for reopened or transferred cases. A 13-day average can conceal a long tail of cases that remain unresolved for months. Timeliness matters; so does the quality of the answer.

## 2. CSC access and feedback are the missing bridge between a portal and a citizen

The two reports present CPGRAMS as more than a website. The portal is integrated with the Common Service Centre network, said by DARPG to be available at more than five lakh CSCs and associated with 2.5 lakh Village Level Entrepreneurs. In September, 12,996 grievances were lodged through CSCs. The state report says Karnataka accounted for 54% of that CSC total. The central report also records 92,822 new user registrations across channels and 1,87,362 feedback entries gathered online and through call centres; 1,11,794 were attributed to Central Ministries/Departments and 75,568 to States/UTs.[^1][^2]

This is a meaningful design choice. A grievance channel that assumes a personal smartphone, stable connectivity, literacy in the interface language, and confidence in uploading documents will not be equally reachable to everyone. Local assistance can help people submit a case and navigate a digital process. However, network coverage is not the same as effective access. The reported 12,996 CSC complaints are a count of cases routed through that channel, not a measure of how many people needed help but could not reach it, how much an assisted filing costs, or whether the complaint was recorded accurately.

Karnataka’s 54% share is a signal worth investigating, not a verdict. It could reflect stronger CSC use, state-specific promotion, fewer alternative channels, or reporting differences; the release does not explain the concentration. Similarly, a large number of feedback entries is not equivalent to a high satisfaction score. The published summary does not disclose what share indicated a resolved problem, whether respondents were contacted after disposal, or how feedback affected department rankings. A feedback count without the response distribution is volume, not quality evidence.

The state report adds a human-capacity strand: under the Sevottam Scheme, DARPG says 1,312 training courses reached about 43,738 officers over five financial years through 30 September 2026. It also notes West Bengal completed full integration with CPGRAMS on 22 September. The lesson is that interoperability depends on more than a software connection: frontline staff must understand categories, routing, deadlines, escalation, and the citizen’s right to challenge an inadequate closure. Publishing a public service standard alongside portal integration—and showing compliance by office—would make this layer more accountable than a simple “connected” status.

## 3. Special Campaign 6 connects e-file cleanup to grievance administration, but closure counts need a service lens

A DFPD mid-campaign report posted on 16 October gives a concrete view of administrative digitisation during Special Campaign 6, which runs from 2 to 31 October. As of 15 October, the Department reported 35,743 physical files reviewed against a target of 1,10,600; 13,546 e-files put up for review and 7,040 closed against a target of 24,879. Its progress table records 710 in the public-grievance row against a target of 1,479 and 29 in the appeals row against a target of 135; it also records 48 against a target of 54 Members of Parliament references and 694 of 2,855 planned campaigns conducted. The department said the work generated ₹10.76 lakh and freed 23,843 square feet of office space.[^3]

The figures show why governance infrastructure cannot be reduced to an app or portal. A grievance can move through an online case record and still depend on a staff member finding the relevant file, reconciling a paper record, seeking a decision, and communicating a useful answer. E-file review and closure can reduce administrative friction; digitised records can make decisions easier to retrieve and audit. The parallel reporting of grievances, appeals, references, and files suggests an attempt to manage both outward-facing service and the internal work behind it.

But “e-file closed” is an internal workflow measure, not a synonym for a complaint resolved. The DFPD release does not say that the 7,040 e-file closures were all CPGRAMS matters, and it would be wrong to equate them. Nor does it explain the criteria used to close an e-file, the age or subject of the files, whether records were digitised before disposal, or what quality checks prevent a case from being marked complete prematurely. The same caution applies to the grievance and appeal rows: the release’s summary table does not distinguish resolved, transferred, rejected, or pending cases.

The targets give a useful progress snapshot but also show substantial work remaining at mid-month. For example, the table records 710 against a public-grievance target of 1,479 and 29 against an appeal target of 135 by 15 October, while the campaign was scheduled to end on the 31st. It does not define what counted as completion in those rows. The next report should state how many were ultimately resolved, how many appealed, and whether records remained searchable after old files were weeded. A digitisation drive is successful when it makes a public decision retrievable and correctable—not simply when the file count falls.

## 4. Dharamshala’s e-governance conference puts interoperability and cybersecurity in the same frame

DARPG and the Himachal Pradesh government scheduled the National Conference on e-Governance Reforms in Dharamshala for 16–17 October. The announced programme covers citizen-centred services, digital transformation, AI, cybersecurity, data analytics, and replication of initiatives. Presentations were to include AgriStack, e-Jagriti, AI-enabled clinical decision support in eSanjeevani, a multi-hazard early-warning system, and iGOT Karmayogi. The day-two agenda included cybersecurity, digital banking, blood-bag traceability, education, urban governance, and ONDC. PIB reported that the two-day conference began in Dharamshala on 16 October; as of this edition’s cutoff, the available material is an announcement and event report, not a published evaluation of conference outcomes.[^4][^5]

The agenda is notable because it places grievance and administrative systems beside the sectoral rails that generate records and service decisions. A complaint about a welfare entitlement, a health service, or a commerce platform may cross departmental boundaries; data exchange and common identifiers can speed a response, but can also propagate a bad record. Cybersecurity therefore belongs in the service-delivery conversation, not in a separate technical session after launch. “Replicating” a model across states also requires evidence that local languages, workflows, staffing, and appeal routes are supported—not merely that the software can be copied.

The conference may help officials compare practices, but the announcement does not show which systems were independently tested, what citizen outcomes they achieved, or what commitments were adopted. The useful follow-up is publication of session material and action points, plus common reporting on uptime, exclusion, complaint age, correction time, accessibility, security incidents, and appeal outcomes. Without such evidence, a conference can catalogue digital initiatives without telling residents whether they made a service more dependable.

## Cross-layer implications

This week’s L6 story is a chain: an accessible intake channel, a correctly routed digital case, a searchable administrative record, a competent decision-maker, and an appeal when the answer fails. CSCs connect public-service access to connectivity and local assistance; interdepartmental platforms connect grievances to identity, sectoral records, and data exchange; e-file workflows shape whether officials can act; cybersecurity protects the entire chain. Any one weak link can turn a “disposed” case into a citizen still waiting.

**What to watch next:** CPGRAMS should publish disposal definitions and outcome-oriented feedback, not only volume; States/UTs should report backlog age and appeals consistently; CSC use should be evaluated for affordability and successful resolution; Special Campaign 6 should disclose final e-file and grievance outcomes; and the Dharamshala conference should release proceedings and measurable follow-up commitments. These are the tests that distinguish more digital administration from better public administration.
