# DPI Deep Dive — Friday | November 06, 2026

**Focus Layer:** L5 — Sectoral Infrastructure: agriculture, health and justice services  
**Coverage Period:** October 30 – November 6, 2026

## Executive summary

This week the sectoral layer ran into its hardest test: people whose relationship to land, court or clinic does not match what the database assumes. The Centre confirmed that tenant farmers and sharecroppers *can* be included in the AgriStack Farmer Registry — but only if a state chooses to onboard them — while fieldwork from Andhra Pradesh's Adivasi areas found that only about half of individual forest-rights title-holders have managed to register for a Farmer ID at all, tripped up by joint titles recorded under a single name, outdated records and pending claims. In justice, the government trained its own officials on eFiling 3.0 at Vigyan Bhawan — the demand side of e-courts, days after a parliamentary committee put the whole layer under appraisal — and the Jharkhand High Court got a ₹35-crore data centre plus six citizen-facing digital services, launched by the Chief Justice of India himself. In health, the Union Health Secretary directed Delhi's Arogya Mandirs to start issuing Baal ABHA IDs for children in routine service delivery, while in Ghaziabad, fourteen medical officers were served notices for conducting zero e-Sanjeevani teleconsultations in a month. The common thread across all five stories: issuance has been solved, and the layer is now being judged on inclusion of the edge cases, adoption by its own operators, and actual utilisation.

## Key developments

### 1. AgriStack opens a lane for tenant farmers — but the states hold the keys

On November 5, reporting on the Centre's AgriStack initiative noted that the Farmer Registry has explicit provisions to include tenant farmers and sharecroppers — the cultivators who till land owned by others under tenancy, lease or sharecropping arrangements and who have historically been invisible to government assistance because their names do not appear on ownership records.[^1]

The design logic is straightforward: the registry maintains information on farmers, land records and agricultural activity so schemes can be delivered to whoever actually cultivates. Registration runs through the AgriStack or state Farmer Registry portal with Aadhaar-linked mobile OTP verification, and tenant farmers may be required to submit valid lease documents or an agreement with the landowner as proof of cultivation, depending on state guidelines.[^1]

But the crucial qualifier deserves the headline. The Centre has clarified that inclusion is **not an automatic nationwide entitlement** — individual state governments decide whether to onboard tenant cultivators, based on their own policies, and access to crop insurance, loans, subsidies and disaster relief will still depend on scheme-level eligibility rules.[^1] A farmer's ability to hold an agricultural identity now depends on which side of a state boundary the plot sits.

This provision is the direct answer to the design flaw flagged when AgriStack crossed 11 crore Farmer IDs five months ahead of schedule last month: IDs are generated by synchronising farmer details with digitised land records, and the tiller is frequently not the person on the record. Making tenant inclusion a state option rather than a national floor is a political choice with distributional consequences. It also embeds a quiet paperwork test: informal tenancy is informal precisely because it is undocumented, and demanding lease deeds may filter out the very farmers the provision targets. States that accept documented-but-flexible proof — self-declaration, gram panchayat attestation — will include more cultivators than states that insist on registered lease deeds.

The contrast case is Madhya Pradesh, the state the Centre showcases as its AgriStack leader, which has now enrolled over 1.2 crore farmers.[^2] Scale at the top is proven. Nothing in the public domain says what share of that number are tenants, sharecroppers, or women — and until state dashboards publish composition, the leaderboard measures coverage, not inclusion.

### 2. "Missing only from the database": Adivasi forest-rights titles collide with the Farmer Registry

The sharpest accountability document the layer received this week came not from government but from fieldwork. Writing in The India Forum on November 3, Chakradhar Buddha and Rahul Mukkera of LibTech India documented what happens when AgriStack meets individual forest rights (IFR) titles in Andhra Pradesh's tribal areas — and the answer is systematic exclusion.[^3]

The mechanics matter. Under Section 4(4) of the Forest Rights Act, 2006, an IFR title is a *household* entitlement that must be registered jointly in the names of the husband and wife. Yet when these titles were digitised, many were recorded under a single name — often one brother in a family of four cultivating together, or the male head of the household. The co-holders do not physically lose the land, but they lose recognition in the digital system. And unlike unpartitioned *revenue* lands, where multiple farmers can register and obtain separate Farmer IDs against the same survey number, **no equivalent provision exists for IFR joint titles** — so only one person per household title can register, and it is often the wife who is left out.[^3]

The numbers behind the anecdote are worse. A state-level official told the authors that only about **52% of Andhra Pradesh's IFR title-holders had registered for a Farmer ID as of July 2026** — against 2.27 lakh IFR titles issued in the state by June 2026. Officially, 1,525 IFR and community forest rights claims remain pending in Andhra Pradesh; but four civil-society organisations working in just 15 gram panchayats reported roughly 800 pending claims between them, suggesting the official count badly understates the queue. Farmers with pending claims cannot register at all, because registration demands a recognised land title.[^3]

The registration friction is layered on top. IFR titles were created through a different administrative process and do not fit the survey-number-based retrieval that Farmer ID registration assumes; the unique digital identifiers assigned to these records live in an online repository but not on the physical titles, so farmers need a smartphone, network coverage, and an Aadhaar-linked mobile number — two of the three registration OTPs go to that mobile — just to locate their own land in the system. Many farmers, and even some frontline functionaries, could not find the number.[^3]

Why this matters beyond one state: Farmer ID registration is already **mandatory for new PM-KISAN enrolments in 19 states**, AgriStack carries an outlay of ₹2,817 crore, and neither the Farmer Registry's official FAQs nor the union government's handbook to states mentions the Forest Rights Act at all. There is also no documented role for the gram sabha — the institution the FRA itself empowers to recognise forest rights — when underlying records are incomplete, outdated or disputed.[^3] If AgriStack becomes the gateway to schemes and formal credit, digitisation defects convert silently into benefit exclusions: there is no rejection letter, only absence from the database. The authors' fix list is concrete and testable — process pending claims, publish clear IFR record-update procedures, put digital identifiers on physical titles, allow separate Farmer IDs for all joint title-holders, and give gram sabhas an explicit role.[^3]

### 3. eFiling 3.0 goes to school: the state trains its own litigants

On November 4, the Department of Justice organised a workshop titled "eFiling 3.0: From Process to Practice" at Vigyan Bhawan, under the eCourts Mission Mode Project, with Minister of State for Law and Justice Arjun Ram Meghwal as chief guest and members of the Supreme Court's eCommittee in attendance. NIC ran the technical sessions: eCourts applications and services, the eCourts Single Sign-On, the eCourts Services mobile app, and eFiling 3.0 features — including provisions specifically for government pleaders and government departments.[^4]

The stated rationale is the interesting part. Ministries, departments, PSUs and autonomous bodies are among the biggest litigants in the country, and the workshop materials acknowledge that eFiling adoption across government is **non-uniform**. The fix being pushed is integration rather than exhortation: eOffice credentials now work with eCourts SSO so designated officers can file and track cases end-to-end, pay fees online via ePay, and initiate cases from anywhere. Officials also framed AI as an enabling tool, not a replacement for human roles.[^5]

The timing gives this workshop its weight. Last week, the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Personnel, Public Grievances, Law and Justice added the eCourts MMP, digitisation and the use of AI in justice delivery to its appraisal agenda for the year. A week later, the executive is at Vigyan Bhawan standardising the demand side of the same system. That sequence sets up the metric that should now be public: **per-ministry and per-PSU e-filing shares**, not the cumulative 1.25 crore cases e-filed since inception, which is an input count that can grow while a specific ministry keeps filing in paper. Phase III's goal of universal eFiling across all courts will be won or lost in the government's own filing rooms first.[^4]

Also this week, the Supreme Court on November 3 allowed a petitioner to place a representation before its eCommittee seeking uniformity in case classification and nomenclature across High Courts — a small procedural step aimed at a real problem: the same case type carries different names and classifications in different High Courts, burdening litigants and fraying the comparability of the National Judicial Data Grid.[^6]

### 4. Justice infrastructure moves down-state: Jharkhand HC's ₹35-crore data centre

On the evening of November 2, Chief Justice of India Surya Kant inaugurated the Jharkhand High Court's first dedicated data centre at the High Court campus in Dhurwa, Ranchi, built at an estimated cost of ₹35 crore, and launched six digital services in one go: a new official website for the court, an iOS application covering the High Court and district courts, and an online portal for obtaining certified copies of court documents and orders, among others. Justice Ananda Sen, chairperson of the court's computerisation committee, described the facility as the first of its kind in Jharkhand and said it would strengthen the technological foundation of the High Court and subordinate courts.[^7]

The CJI used the occasion to press the broader point: the judicial system needs to be more accessible and efficient through modern technology. A Bar Council member quoted at the event framed the citizen stakes — reliable information, transparency and speedy justice for people seeking legal remedies.[^7]

Two features distinguish this from routine launch coverage. First, **compute ownership is moving to the High Court level**. Judicial data in India has largely lived on national NIC infrastructure; a state High Court running its own data centre is a governance shift, and with it come questions that should be asked now rather than after an incident: who audits the facility, what are its security certifications, and how does it interoperate with the national stack. Second, the certified-copies portal is the launch item a litigant will actually touch. e-filing and NJDG dashboards get headlines, but certified copies are where citizens meet the system's paperwork — the honest test is end-to-end digital delivery and turnaround time. Six launches in one evening is a bundle; watch which services publish usage statistics and survive their first year.[^7]

### 5. Health's two-front week: Baal ABHA for children, zero-consultation notices for doctors

On November 5, Union Health Secretary Punya Salila Srivastava reviewed three Ayushman Arogya Mandirs in Delhi — the urban primary health centres at Babar Road and Trade & Taxes, and the urban sub-health centre at Golf Links — and issued directions that quietly extend the health-DPI identity layer to a new population: **Baal Ayushman Bharat Health Account (ABHA) IDs for children**, to be created as part of routine service delivery at facilities, to support continuity of care through digitally accessible records and closer ABDM integration. She also directed that National Quality Assurance Standards certification be initiated for urban sub-health centres under the National Urban Health Mission, and fixed weekly yoga and wellness sessions at all Arogya Mandirs.[^8]

The counterpoint arrived the next day from Ghaziabad. The Times of India reported on November 6 that at least **14 medical officers in the district conducted zero teleconsultations** under the Centre's e-Sanjeevani scheme in September — against a prescribed monthly target of 60 per officer — and are being served notices by the district health department, led by Chief Medical Officer Dr Sachin Vaish. The report noted the data has raised concerns over implementation of the national telemedicine service.[^9]

Read together, these are one story told from both ends. At the policy end, the identity layer keeps extending — now to children, which is sensible: immunisation, growth monitoring and paediatric care are precisely where a longitudinal record pays off, and a childhood ABHA created at first contact is the entry point to a lifetime health record. At the district end, the teleconsultation layer has entered its enforcement phase: the issuance numbers are met, so the administration now polices utilisation with notices and monthly targets.

Targets produce activity, though, not necessarily care. The risk with quota-style enforcement is the logged-but-empty consultation — activity that satisfies the dashboard without serving a patient. The honest metrics are consultation completion rates, follow-up linkage, and patient-side experience — and whether districts publish their e-Sanjeevani utilisation data openly or leave citizens to learn about zero-consultation months through newspaper reports of disciplinary notices. The same transparency question that hangs over Farmer ID error rates and e-filing adoption hangs over teleconsultation counts: the data exists; it is the publication that is discretionary.

## Trends and implications

- **"Who is the farmer?" has become a definitional battleground.** The tenant provision, the IFR field audit and Madhya Pradesh's 1.2 crore enrolments describe a registry that has mastered issuance and is now arguing over who counts. The edge cases — tenants, women co-holders, forest-rights households — are not rounding errors; they are a large share of actual cultivators.
- **Land records are the single point of failure for agricultural DPI.** Both agri stories trace exclusion to defects in the legacy record: joint titles flattened to one name, fathers' names never updated, claims pending for years. AgriStack inherits the defects of the paper it digitised — which makes correction and update capacity, not enrolment drives, the core infrastructure function of the coming phase.
- **Government-as-litigant is eCourts' new adoption frontier.** A week after the standing committee put the layer under appraisal, the executive trained its own officers on eFiling 3.0 and admitted non-uniform adoption. Per-ministry filing data is the metric to demand.
- **Justice infrastructure is decentralising down-state.** HC-level data centres and certified-copy portals shift the layer's stack closer to litigants — and shift audit, security and interoperability questions closer too.
- **The health layer has begun enforcing use, not just issuing identity.** Baal ABHA at first contact and zero-consultation notices are two ends of the same enforcement phase. Whether that enforcement is data-transparent or paper-anecdotal decides if citizens can hold the system to account.

## What to watch next

1. Which states operationalise tenant-farmer onboarding in the Farmer Registry, what proof of cultivation they accept, and whether Madhya Pradesh publishes the tenant share within its 1.2 crore.
2. Whether the Agriculture Ministry publishes national IFR-holder registration rates — Andhra Pradesh's 52% should not remain a figure only a field researcher can cite — and whether Farmer Registry guidance is amended to reference the FRA and gram sabha roles.
3. Post-workshop eFiling 3.0 adoption: per-ministry and per-PSU electronic filing shares, and whether the standing committee's appraisal demands them.
4. Jharkhand HC's certified-copies turnaround times and portal usage, plus whether other High Courts follow the dedicated data-centre model.
5. Whether e-Sanjeevani's zero-consultation enforcement spreads to other districts, and whether consultation-quality metrics accompany the quantity targets.
