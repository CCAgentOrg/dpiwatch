# DPI Deep Dive — Saturday | November 21, 2026

**Focus Layer:** L6 — Governance & Grievance: DARPG, CPGRAMS, eOffice
**Coverage Period:** November 14 – November 21, 2026

## Executive summary

The governance layer's week was a week of scorecards, and the scorecards disagree with each other in productive ways. On November 18, DARPG released its October monthly CPGRAMS reports — the 54th for Central Ministries and the 51st for States/UTs — showing disposal outrunning receipts for another month and pendency falling from August's peak, alongside a Grievance Redressal Assessment & Index that quietly reformed itself in February by removing score normalization to make its arithmetic "easily verifiable."[^1][^2] Yet the same report discloses that of 88,417 citizen feedbacks received in October, only 37.1% were resolved — and satisfaction is published as a percentage *of the resolved*, not of the citizens who complained.[^2] Meanwhile the labour queue tells a harder story: the Ministry of Labour and Employment absorbed 37,472 grievances in October — more than a third of all central receipts — in the same fortnight that the NREGA Sangharsh Morcha publicly charged that employment grievances filed on CPGRAMS from six states were "falsely closed without any action."[^2][^3] Around these, the week delivered the layer's characteristic instruments in action: a Comptroller-side order (CGA Order No 3827, November 18) pushing daily CPGRAMS monitoring down into the Pay & Accounts Offices,[^4] the launch of the PM Awards 2026 portal on November 13 with 2,000+ officers from 750 locations dialing in,[^5] and eOffice's diffusion week — Kashmir's school-education directorate mandating e-correspondence by November 15, and the Inland Waterways Authority ordering Aadhaar-based e-sign inside its eOffice workflow on November 16.[^6][^7] The connective thread: India's grievance apparatus has become superb at producing *proof of process* — disposal counts, indices, monitoring orders, campaign tallies — while the one number that measures whether citizens got redress (the unresolved-feedback share, 62.9% in October) is disclosed but not indexed, incentivized or enforced.[^2]

## Key developments

### 1. The October scorecard: receipts near record pace, disposal outrunning them — and a pendency that won't die

DARPG's 54th CPGRAMS Monthly Report for Central Ministries (October 2026), released November 18, records 2,24,436 grievances received, 2,35,082 redressed, and a closing pendency of 78,076 — the 52nd consecutive month in which Central Secretariat disposals crossed one lakh.[^1][^2] The trajectory beneath the headline is worth reading closely. September's receipts hit 2,51,147 — the year's peak — before October's intake eased back to 2,24,436; pendency, which peaked at 93,864 in August, fell to 78,076 by October, with roughly 68% of the pending stock less than 21 days old, the age threshold set by DARPG's 2024 Comprehensive Guidelines for Grievance Redressal.[^2] The appeal mechanism is carrying real load too: 42,669 appeals received and 42,818 disposed in October, against an appeal pendency of 23,081.[^2] On the States/UTs side, the 51st Monthly Report shows 95,430 grievances received and 1,05,617 redressed in October — again, redressal outrunning intake.[^3]

The quality instrument is the Grievance Redressal Assessment & Index (GRAI), scored monthly on four dimensions — Efficiency (0.45 weight), Feedback (0.30), Domain (0.15) and Organisational Commitment (0.10). October's Group A leaders were the Department of Posts, CBIC and the Department of Telecommunications; Group B's were the Department of Financial Services (Pension Reforms), the Ministry of Tribal Affairs and AYUSH.[^2] Two design facts make GRAI more interesting than a ranking. First, since February 2026 DARPG has removed normalization from the score calculation "to make the GRAI Score Calculation easily verifiable" — a rare instance of a government index simplifying its own arithmetic for auditability rather than complicating it.[^2] Second, GRAI still contains a Feedback dimension worth 30% even though — as the next story shows — what "feedback" measures is not citizen satisfaction with the portal overall. An index is only as honest as its numerator, and DARPG's numerator is still chosen by the measured.

### 2. The feedback gap: 62.9% of October's citizen feedback closed as "not resolved"

The most consequential number in the 54th report is one DARPG discloses but does not headline. In October 2026, Ministries received 88,417 items of citizen feedback through the call centre and online channels. Of these, 32,810 — 37.1% — were marked resolved; 55,607, or 62.9%, were *not resolved*. Satisfaction is then reported as ~80% — but read the denominator: 26,258 of the 32,810 *resolved* cases expressed satisfaction. Citizens who complained and never got resolution (62.9% of feedback-givers) sit entirely outside that satisfaction figure.[^2] The cumulative January–October picture is the same shape: 6,99,593 feedbacks received, 2,86,429 resolved (40.9%), and 2,28,879 satisfied — roughly 5.6 lakh citizens across ten months whose feedback ended either unresolved or unsatisfactory.[^2]

This is the governance layer's structural asymmetry in miniature. Disposal counts — 2,35,082 in October — are aggregated, ranked, indexed and awarded. The unresolved-feedback share is disclosed in a 74-page PDF that nobody indexes. A citizen whose grievance is "closed" without satisfaction does not disappear from the system's math; they simply migrate out of every metric that carries consequences. GRAI's Feedback dimension, worth 30%, measures whether ministries responded to feedback, not whether feedback-givers got redress. The reform DARPG applied to GRAI in February — making the arithmetic verifiable — is exactly the instinct the feedback table needs next: publish the *unresolved* share per ministry in the index, and the incentive structure changes overnight. Anything else measures the bureaucracy's diary, not the citizen's outcome.

### 3. Labour's 37,472 grievances meet the "falsely closed" charge

The layer's hardest story of the week is the collision between the biggest grievance queue and an organized claim that the queue's closures are hollow. The 54th report shows the Ministry of Labour and Employment as October's largest grievance destination — 37,472 grievances, over 36% of all central receipts — ahead of the Department of Financial Services (Banking) at 32,051 and DoT at 11,357.[^2] On November 20, the NREGA Sangharsh Morcha — a coalition of rural-workers' organizations backing the Samyukta Kisan Morcha's November 26 protest call — published a detailed account asserting that employment under VB-GRAMG (the successor to MGNREGA) is not reaching workers and that wages are unpaid in specific villages of Uttar Pradesh, Bihar and Maharashtra; it further claims that around 100+ grievances filed on CPGRAMS in September–October from Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Karnataka, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh and Rajasthan were "falsely closed without any action," alongside documented failures of the NMMS attendance app that requires workers to travel to distant worksites to photograph themselves.[^3] The coalition also cites a Financial Express report that the Union government expects ₹50,000 crore in savings under the VB-GRAMG head against roughly ₹5,000 crore of central expenditure so far in FY 2026-27 — a figure that reads as delayed or foregone entitlement spending depending on which side of the counter you stand.[^3]

Our evidence standards apply here: this is an activist claim, not an official finding, and the ₹50,000 crore figure is a projection reported second-hand. But the structural point survives any scrutiny level. The labour ministry's grievance queue — the largest in the country — is processing the grievances of citizens whose statutory entitlement is work and wages, under a scheme whose attendance layer (NMMS) is itself a contested technology. When a grievance portal's unit of output is the *closure*, and closure discipline is enforced by daily monitoring orders and campaign targets (see below), the easy failure mode is closure-without-redress: the case file says resolved, the wage list says otherwise. The appeal mechanism — 42,669 appeals received in October against 23,081 pending — is the system's own acknowledgment that first-instance closures need a second look.[^2] What the apparatus still lacks is the one instrument that would catch false closures at scale: publishing, per ministry, the rate at which closed grievances generate appeals, re-complaints or unresolved feedback. The data already exists inside CPGRAMS; it is the index that hasn't been built.

### 4. The compliance cascade: an OM's journey from DARPG to the Pay & Accounts Office

The week's quietest story is the governance layer's characteristic instrument in motion: the office memorandum. On November 18, the Controller General of Accounts issued Order No 3827 directing compliance with DARPG's office memoranda of August 31, 2015 and August 23, 2024 — the layer's standing grievance-handling guidance, refreshed in 2024 — and instituting *daily* monitoring of the CPGRAMS portal across its pay-and-accounts offices.[^4] The same week, DARPG's capacity-building arm ran an interactive session for Mizoram secretariat officers (November 16) covering CPGRAMS, eOffice and AI-in-governance applications — the training pipeline feeding the same reforms to state capitals.[^8]

Read together, these are the machinery of what might be called grievance discipline: a nodal department writes guidelines; the accounting establishment re-issues them as compliance orders; trainers push them to the periphery; and the daily disposal numbers that result flow back into the monthly report and the index. The Special Campaign 6 results released over the last fortnight complete the picture — CBDT reported 29,153 grievances disposed against a target of 15,081, exceeding its target by 93.3%, alongside 1.54 lakh sq ft of freed office space; DPIIT reported over 99% grievance disposal as of October 28.[^9][^10] A 93% over-achievement on a grievance-disposal target is worth pausing on: it means either the target was set for a campaign that had already been won, or disposal is elastic enough to be surged on demand. Both possibilities say something true about campaign-mode governance — the time-boxed, all-hands disposal drive is the production function this layer knows best. Its known failure mode is the one story three describes: when disposal is the deliverable, quality is the externality. The CGA's daily-monitoring order and CBDT's campaign tally are process-proof; the unresolved-feedback table is the outcome-proof — and only one of the two travels up the hierarchy.

### 5. The incentive layer: PM Awards 2026 and eOffice's diffusion week

DARPG's third instrument is the award. The PM Awards for Excellence in Public Administration 2026 portal (pmawards.gov.in) launched November 13 through a video-conference pulling in more than 2,000 officers from 750 locations; 16 awards are planned, each carrying a trophy, a scroll and a ₹20 lakh incentive, for work delivered between April 1, 2024 and November 30, 2026.[^5][^11] The Holistic Development of Districts category was comprehensively revamped with nine new schemes added — Swachh Bharat (Urban) and AMRUT, Gyan Bharatam, PM Matsya Sampada Yojana, TB Mukt Bharat, Crop Diversification, PM SHRI, Swachh Bharat (Grameen), the National Mission on Natural Farming, and capacity-building under iGOT — with three 2025 schemes retained (Har Ghar Jal, PM Surya Ghar, Saksham Anganwadi & Poshan 2.0) and the approved set rolling forward into the 2027 awards. The Aspirational Blocks category will cover 513 blocks across 331 districts; ministries were asked to submit evaluation parameters by November 17.[^11] Conspicuously absent from the award categories: grievance redressal itself. The department that runs CPGRAMS rewards districts for implementing schemes, not for resolving grievances — the diagnostic instrument and the incentive instrument never meet. A "GRAI-linked" award category would cost DARPG nothing but its own data and would be the cheapest reform in the entire governance stack.

Meanwhile eOffice — the layer's process backbone — had a diffusion week. Kashmir's Directorate of School Education ordered mandatory initiation of official correspondence through eOffice by November 15, on a cloud-enabled platform hosted at the state data centre,[^6] and the Inland Waterways Authority of India issued a November 16 office order governing the use of digital signatures and *Aadhaar-based e-sign* inside its eOffice workflows.[^7] The IWAI order is a cross-layer artifact: the identity layer (L1) has become the signature infrastructure of the administrative state, with Aadhaar authentication executing what pen and stamp used to. Assam provides the trajectory line — the state has made paperless eOffice mandatory from January 1, 2027, with all 58 departments at its secretariat already live on the NIC platform.[^12] The paperless office's second phase — out of the central secretariat, into directorates, districts and school-education administrations — is now visibly underway, and it is being driven not by platform launches but by exactly these small, dated, enforceable orders.

## Cross-layer connections

- **L1 — Identity:** IWAI's Aadhaar-based e-sign order makes the identity layer the signing authority of governance workflows; the paperless office's integrity now depends on eKYC and authentication hygiene, not just file management.[^7]
- **L2 — Payments:** DFS (Banking) at 32,051 grievances and MoLE at 37,472 make the grievance queue a real-time sensor for payment-fraud and wage-DBT failures; CPGRAMS category data is an underused open dataset for the payments layer's fraud monitoring.[^2]
- **L5 — Sectoral:** the NSM's NMMS attendance complaints are a sectoral-infrastructure failure (biometric attendance for rural works) expressing itself as a governance-layer grievance-backlog story; the layers are one system from the worker's side.[^3]
- **L7 — Trust & Privacy:** GRAI's February 2026 removal of normalization for verifiability is the transparency instinct this layer needs to apply to its feedback table; a portal that publishes its own unverifiable satisfaction share is a trust liability waiting for an RTI.[^2]
- **L4 — Commerce:** eOffice's spread into directorates and district administrations is quietly the largest B2G digitization program in the country — suppliers, tender files and establishment papers all move on it; its uptime and audit trail are commerce infrastructure.[^6][^12]
